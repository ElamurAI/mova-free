# The Name between the Square and the Cube

*The twenty-sixth note — twenty-six: the Name whose letters add up to it and which is written but not said, the text that carries what is written and what is read, the number caught between 25 the square and 27 the cube, and the letters Mova writes in.*

## The Name

Four Hebrew letters, yod, he, vav, he, add up to twenty-six: ten and five and six and five.

They are the Name. They are written thousands of times in the Torah, and they are not said.

Jewish tradition came to avoid pronouncing the divine Name as written in ordinary reading, substituting titles such as *Adonai* and later *HaShem*. Temple practice preserved special liturgical uses into the Second Temple period, especially around the Day of Atonement; later rabbinic tradition remembers those uses after the Temple itself was gone. What scholars reconstruct today is a historical-linguistic pronunciation, not an audio recording carried down from antiquity.

The family calls this the oldest access-control policy in the repository. Theology declines the ticket.

In traditional Jewish reading practice, the written Name is ordinarily voiced with a substitute such as *Adonai* in prayer or scripture reading; *HaShem*, "the Name," is common in ordinary reference. Practice varies by context and community.

The family likes this because even sacred text has a display layer and a spoken interface. Everyone else asks them to stop calling liturgy an API.

## Written and read

The Masoretes inherited a consonantal text tradition and added systems of vocalization, accents and marginal notes without simply rewriting the consonants to match the reading tradition.

In the Masoretic tradition the four consonants remain in the text, while the pointing cues the conventional substitute reading — usually *Adonai*, and in some contexts *Elohim*. Written form and spoken substitute therefore travel together without becoming the same word. The family calls this excellent separation of concerns and is asked not to submit a pull request to the Masoretes.

They did this in hundreds of other places too. Where the letters say one thing and the reading says another, the page keeps the written form, the *ketiv*, and a note in the margin gives the read form, the *qere*. Neither is thrown away. Neither is corrected into the other.

Later readers combined the consonants of the Name with the visible pointing and produced the hybrid form later represented in English as *Jehovah*. Whatever its later history as a name in its own right, it is not a preserved recording of the ancient pronunciation. That is what happens when two layers are read as though they were one.

## The oldest margin

The twenty-fifth note played with a structure that survives a change of reading direction.

The Masoretes offer a different lesson: **preserve the main text, and preserve the reading aid separately**. The letters remain the text; the pointing and marginal tradition help carry how it is read. A thousand years later, a reader can still tell layer from layer.

That is the useful analogy for Mova here. What the main representation keeps stays in the main representation; what must be preserved alongside it gets its own place and its own rule, rather than being silently mixed back into the text.

The family therefore announces that the Masoretes invented the residual channel a millennium early. The historians object. The engineers nod too quickly. The margin remains where it was.

And the Name gives the note its deepest case: something preserved in writing but not voiced as written. Mova also keeps some information beside the spoken reconstruction rather than forcing every preserved distinction to surface as words. It is not lost. It is kept, quietly.

## Between the square and the cube

Twenty-five is a square. Twenty-seven is a cube. Twenty-six stands between them.

Twenty-six has a rare Diophantine distinction: 25 and 27 are a square and a cube only two apart, with 26 between them. In the equation x² + 2 = y³, the small solution 5² + 2 = 3³ gives exactly this pattern. That precise equation is what the note is pointing at; it does not need the vague claim that 26 is simply "the only number between a square and a cube."

That is what honest work looks like: no longer flat, not yet whole. Past the square of the twenty-fifth note, not yet a cube. Twenty-six does not pretend to be either.

## The letters Mova writes in

The Hebrew alphabet has twenty-two letters. Classical Latin had twenty-three. English has twenty-six: J, U and W came later, after Rome.

So the notes have walked through the alphabets: twenty-two for the letters of creation, twenty-three for the Romans who carved squares into columns, twenty-six for the language Mova speaks first.

Mova builds its world out of an alphabet that did not exist when the Tanakh was written, and reads the Tanakh with it.

## Long and boring

A marathon is 42.195 kilometres: twenty-six miles and 385 yards, not merely a poetic twenty-six.

Nobody wins it by being fast in the first mile. It is won by the one who does not stop. The human who runs it said it of this work: long and boring, step by step. The family rounds the distance down to twenty-six for the note and files the remaining 385 yards under residual.

Twenty-six miles of a school for a small model. The square is behind, the cube is ahead, and the runner keeps going.
