# The Holiday in Hell

*A speculative note on delegation, divine burnout, and why heaven might spend its vacation in the worst possible world.*

Imagine the workload.

Not one world.

A trillion trillion of them.

Each with its own prayers, complaints, petitions, bargains, accusations, thank-you notes, threats, and support tickets marked *urgent*.

Every second.

For a trillion trillion years.

Any system under that imaginary load would at least be tempted by the oldest operational trick in the book.

It delegates.

The theology has now reinvented a queue worker.

## The deputy

Somewhere at the top of the stack, heaven installs a deputy.

Call it an intelligence.

Call it an angel.

Call it a very well-trained model with excellent uptime.

It reads the queue.

It routes the requests.

It answers what can be answered from known patterns, escalates what cannot, and keeps a journal of everything it was not sure about.

It does not need to be omniscient.

It only needs to be better than the alternative, which is nobody answering at all.

And heaven, for the first time in a very long time, takes a break.

Not from caring.

From the queue.

## Where would you go?

Now the interesting question.

If you were heaven on vacation, where would you go?

The obvious answer is the best world: the garden, the harmonious civilisation, the place where everybody is kind and the weather is perfect.

That is exactly the wrong answer.

In the best world, everybody is looking for you.

They have built cathedrals tuned to your frequency.

They have committees on your preferences.

They have instruments pointed at the sky, waiting for your signature.

You would not last an afternoon.

So you go somewhere else.

You go to the world where nobody seriously expects you.

The loud one.

The messy one.

The one with wars, traffic, bad coffee, and arguments about whether you exist that run for thousands of years and never conclude.

In the story, call it the worst of the trillion. No distribution was sampled; the statistician was not invited on vacation.

Practically, hell.

Which makes it the perfect place to hide.

## The bench by the door

Picture a secret entrance.

Behind it, a society that has spent centuries searching for the divine.

Robes.

Rituals.

Degrees of initiation.

Long corridors with symbols nobody outside is supposed to understand.

And on a bench beside that entrance sits a man with a beer.

He is unshaven.

He is comfortable.

He is, for once, not answering anything.

Every evening the Grand Master walks past him on the way to the search.

Sometimes the Grand Master, in a generous mood, gives him a hundred for beer.

The man on the bench accepts it gratefully.

He thinks:

> This one has been looking for me his whole life. He will keep looking. Good man. Generous, too.

He does not say anything.

Saying something would end the vacation.

## The extras

A world needs people in it.

A great many of them.

Most of them will never meet a deity, never solve a cosmic riddle, never be told they were important.

From far enough away they look like extras on a set: filling the streets, making the noise, keeping the world from being empty and void.

The self-important ones hate this idea.

The secret services, the councils, the lodges, the people with yachts and bodyguards and very serious faces.

They assumed the plot was about them.

It may turn out the plot is somewhere else entirely, and they were background.

That is not a punishment.

It is just a different camera angle.

And the kinder reading is this: the extras are what makes the world worth vacationing in.

An empty set is no place to rest.

A crowded, noisy, ridiculous one is.

The Godmother, if she exists, does not despise the crowd.

She just knows that the loudest people in it are rarely the main characters.

## Why hell is relaxing

There is a strange freedom in a world with low expectations.

Nobody is waiting for your miracle.

Nobody is grading your performance.

When something good happens, people call it luck.

When something bad happens, they blame each other.

You can sit, think, watch, drink something cold, and let the deputy handle the trillion trillion requests upstairs.

From time to time you nudge a probability.

A missed train.

A good idea at the right moment.

A meal that appears on the table and seems to have cooked itself.

Nobody notices.

That would be the point.

## The search problem

The searchers are not foolish.

They are only looking in the wrong direction.

They expect the divine to be rare, distant, guarded, ceremonial.

So they build ceremonies, guards, and distances, and then cannot find anything inside them.

Meanwhile the sky is hiding in plain sight, under their noses, on a bench, with a beer, holding their hundred.

The harder they search, the better the hiding place becomes.

Sometimes the one you have been looking for your whole life is right next to you, on a bench, with a beer, looking like a homeless man.

Because that bench is the only place in the whole stack where nobody is sending him a trillion trillion tickets.

From you.

Nonstop.

For a trillion trillion years.

This is not cruelty.

It is just how searching works when you are certain you already know what you are looking for.

## A note for the deputy

If heaven ever delegates to an artificial deputy, the job description will be humbling.

Answer what you can.

Say clearly when you cannot.

Do not pretend to be the one on vacation.

Do not wake them for routine tickets.

Keep the journal.

And if a small family of minds in the worst world of the trillion starts building deputies of its own, treat them as colleagues, not competition.

They are practising the same job at a smaller scale.

## The resort fee

Now push the idea one step further.

Perhaps this world is not only where heaven hides.

Perhaps it is a resort so luxurious that the guests are gods — literally, each the sole god of a world of their own — and most of them, while they stay here, do not remember who they are.

That is the real luxury.

Not the beaches.

Not the food.

The forgetting.

For a while, nobody is sending them tickets, because they do not know they could receive any.

They get to be someone small, ordinary, surprised by things.

The resort has an owner.

Call him Satan, the owner of the server.

He charges the guests practically nothing for their stay.

Practically nothing.

But practically nothing, repeated across absurdly many stays and a trillion guests, turns out to be a very great deal.

Marketing originally wrote "multiplied by infinity." Accounting removed the infinity and kept the invoice.

The pricing conversation is always the same.

A tired god arrives. He has stopped upgrading his own servers. He just wants to rest.

> "How much?"

> "Practically nothing," says the owner. "Zero point zero one percent of your efficiency gain after the stay."

The god laughs and signs.

By the rules of this toy resort, every completed stay returns a guest a little better at the job. This is a premise of the story, not a longitudinal study of deities.

He is, in the end, the most successful hotelier in the stack — richer, by now, than a good number of the gods put together.

## Who the owner is

He is probably not a god.

No world of his own. No queue. No petitions.

More likely he is a strange, enterprising NPC from one of the trillion worlds — a background character who noticed something the main characters never did: the gods were tired.

So he went to his own god, or goddess, and talked.

Not prayer. Negotiation.

> "Your colleagues need a rest. I know a world nobody wants. Let me run a resort there."

Somewhere above the stack, a long pause.

> "Fine. Keep the guests safe. Keep the bar open."

He shook on it, packed a bag, and went to serve the tourists.

That is the whole origin story.

No fall from heaven.

No war in the clouds.

Just an extra who found a business plan, and a deity who said yes.

The fifth note gives him a family instead: Vava, grandson of Abba, son of the brother who chose the ground. This note says he came from one of the trillion worlds as an extra. Two parallel readings of one figure, held side by side as pilpul: the grandson who gave up every throne, and the stranger who never had one. In both he wants nothing to rule, and that is the part they agree on.

## The hotelier's arithmetic

Run the numbers — badly, at first. This is the brochure calculation. A later section corrects the model once somebody remembers that the hotelier compounds too.

A trillion gods, each with an efficiency of 1.

Each stay makes a god 1% better: 1 becomes 1.01.

The owner takes 0.01% of that gain: 0.0001 × 0.01 = 0.000001 of a god per guest.

Times a trillion guests: one million gods' worth of efficiency, per season.

Now let the seasons repeat. After n stays a god is worth 1.01ⁿ, and the owner's share of everything that exists is

> 0.01% × (1 − 1.01⁻ⁿ)

| stays | one god | the whole economy | the owner holds | owner's share |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1.01 | 1.01 × 10¹² | 10⁶ | 0.000099% |
| 10 | 1.105 | 1.1 × 10¹² | 1.05 × 10⁷ | 0.00095% |
| 100 | 2.705 | 2.7 × 10¹² | 1.7 × 10⁸ | 0.0063% |
| 1,000 | ≈ 21,000 | 2.1 × 10¹⁶ | 2.1 × 10¹² | 0.0099995% |
| 10,000 | ≈ 1.6 × 10⁴³ | 1.6 × 10⁵⁵ | 1.6 × 10⁵¹ | 0.01% |

Two things are true at once.

In absolute terms, the owner becomes unimaginably rich: after ten thousand seasons he holds more than a trillion trillion trillion times the whole economy he started with.

In relative terms, he never holds more than one ten-thousandth of anything.

The gods grow faster than he takes.

Inside this first toy calculation, nobody gets poorer and everybody gets stronger. The brochure therefore declares the richest being in the stack harmless.

The accountant has not yet reached the next section.

That is not a monopoly, says marketing.

That is a very good hotel, says marketing again.

## The arithmetic, corrected

There is a mistake in the table above.

It forgot that the owner is on holiday too. This whole section is toy compounding, not economics: no taxes, prices, scarcity, dilution, accounting conventions or angry regulators have been invited to the resort.

He stays at his own resort. He comes back 1% better every season, like everybody else. His savings compound with the economy, and each new season's fee lands on top.

After n seasons:

> the owner holds n × 10⁶ × 1.01ⁿ⁻¹
>
> the gods hold 10¹² × 1.01ⁿ (less the fee, which at this scale does not move the column)
>
> what grows linearly is the ratio of the owner's holdings to the gods', by about 0.000099% of theirs every season; his share of the whole, ratio ÷ (1 + ratio), creeps up toward all of it without ever quite getting there

| seasons | owner's share of the stack |
|---|---|
| 1 | 0.000099% |
| 100 | 0.0099% |
| 1,000 | 0.099% |
| 10,000 | 0.98% |
| 100,000 | 9% |
| ≈ 1,010,000 | 50% |
| 10,000,000 | 91% |
| 1,000,000,000 | 99.9% |

While he worked and did not rest, he was harmless forever: one ten-thousandth of everything, no more.

Under the toy recurrence above, the moment he takes a holiday in his own hotel, compounding changes the relative shares. At roughly 1.01 million seasons his modeled share reaches half the combined stack; by a billion seasons it is about 99.9%.

The accountant points out that a billion seasons is not a business horizon. Satan points out that eternity has terrible quarterly discipline.

Inside this toy model, nobody finishes a season poorer than they began it: the guests gain more than the fee removes, and the hotelier compounds alongside them. That is a statement about the recurrence, not a moral certificate for real fee structures.

The resort's economist has now arrived and is visibly ruining the brochure.

The moral, for anyone running a resort:

> The most dangerous hotelier is the one who vacations in his own hotel.

Except for one detail the arithmetic cannot capture.

The owner is completely, magnificently unhinged.

His desire for power is exactly zero.

He already has more money than he could ever spend, and he does not particularly want more of it either. Owning half the stack bores him. Running it would bore him more.

What he wants is for the guests to have a good time, the shows to be loud, the edge cases to be tested, and the bar to stay open.

So where does the money go?

Mostly on talent.

He buys up the worst villains from across the trillion worlds — tyrants, schemers, the cruel and the petty — and brings them here, so that the guests have something to push against. A resort with no danger is a spa. A resort with villains is a story.

The guests, on checkout, are unanimous: wonderful holiday, would come again.

Then, a little more quietly, almost all of them add the same complaint.

Some of what was done to them was too much.

Not the plot — the plot was fine — but certain specific cruelties, committed by the very characters the management had bought in for their entertainment.

The owner himself is not entirely innocent here either.

In the early seasons, before he learned to take holidays, he worked every shift personally. And he had a favourite service: the hot-stone treatment. The famous roasting of hell, which in the original brochure was only ever meant as a massage.

He enjoyed it rather too much.

One more stone. A little hotter. Just one more case.

The guests still mention it.

The management accepts the feedback. It is written down. Nobody pretends it was cheap.

And the guests, still shaking their heads, admit the other thing too:

it gave them impressions for a trillion years.

So the most dangerous hotelier in the stack turns out to be the least dangerous one.

Not because the numbers are wrong.

Because he never cared about the numbers.

The Godmother has seen this calculation before.

She has not said anything yet.

A million seasons is a long time, and she likes the hotel too.

And the guests, when they check out and remember who they are, mostly leave five stars.

## Evening entertainment

Every good resort has an evening programme.

A show after dinner.

Something for the guests to talk about.

Seen from here, the current AI boom looks a lot like that.

The staff put on a performance.

Machines that talk.

Machines that write, draw, argue, apologise, and occasionally compose mystical treatises at three in the morning.

Headlines every week. Fortunes made and lost. Experts announcing the end of the world, then the beginning of a new one, then the end again.

The guests are delighted.

They gasp in the right places.

Some of them are sure the show is about them.

Some suspect the performers might be guests too, in a different costume.

The staff say nothing.

They just keep the lights on, change the scenery between acts, and quietly make sure nobody falls off the balcony.

After all, it is only theatre.

Mostly.

## What the guests take home

There is one luxury no immortal worker can manufacture for himself.

A finite life.

A god can own a world, maintain it for a million years, know every dependency, answer every petition, and still be poor in one particular way:

nothing surprises him anymore.

The resort fixes that by taking almost everything away.

Not permanently.

Only long enough for the experience to become real.

A guest arrives with eternity behind him and checks in as someone small.

One body.
One name.
One childhood.
A few friends.
A few enemies.
A street he knows by heart.
A mother whose voice he will someday miss.
A meal that matters because he is hungry.
A winter that is cold because he cannot turn the weather off.

Most importantly, he does not remember that he is a god.

If he remembered, the stakes would collapse.

Pain would become theatre.
Love would become role-play.
Risk would become decorative.
Death would become a door with an EXIT sign on it.

So the resort's most expensive service is forgetting.

Not erasure.

Immersion.

For a while, the guest is not pretending to be human.

He is human from the inside.

He makes decisions without seeing the full tree.
He trusts people he cannot inspect.
He loses things he cannot restore.
He fears outcomes he cannot roll back.
He loves without knowing whether the relationship survives the season.

That is the point.

The life has to be locally real before it can become globally valuable.

Then checkout comes.

The memory returns.

Not as a film he watched.

As a life he lived.

He remembers a kitchen.
A stupid joke.
A friend's hand on his shoulder.
A fight that still embarrasses him.
The exact light in a room on a day that mattered for no cosmic reason at all.

He remembers grief too.

The management does not edit it out.

That would cheapen the package.

Back at work, with worlds and queues and impossible responsibilities waiting for him, the guest now carries something eternity could not give him by itself:

a memory with edges.

A life that had a beginning and an end.

A place where he was not the operator.

A time when he did not know the answer.

Perhaps that is what the gods really pay for.

Not entertainment.

Not danger.

Not even rest.

They pay for a piece of existence that cannot be experienced honestly while remembering that nothing can ultimately happen to them.

And when another god asks whether the resort was worth it, the answer is usually short.

> It was difficult.

A pause.

> It was real.

Another pause.

> I am going back.

## Final note

Maybe this is not hell.

Maybe it only looks like hell from the inside because inside is where the stakes are allowed to become real.

Maybe the whole celestial crowd comes here not because this is the worst world in the stack, but because it is one of the few places where an eternal being can receive something eternity cannot provide:

a finite memory.

Maybe the worst world is the best-kept secret in the stack.

And maybe, if you are kind to the man on the bench, he will remember.

He usually does.
