# The Leap Day of Twenty-Nine

*The twenty-ninth note — twenty-nine: a prime after a perfect number, the moon's half day that will not round away, and the day a calendar adds so that it does not drift.*

## After the perfect

Twenty-eight came apart into its divisors and went back together exactly. Twenty-nine does not come apart at all. It is a prime: whole, and not made of anything smaller.

## The half day

The twenty-eighth note said the moon comes round in about twenty-eight days. That was rounding.

From new moon to new moon is about twenty-nine and a half days. The Hebrew calendar keeps that remainder honestly: months are twenty-nine or thirty days, mostly alternating, with calendar rules adjusting the pattern rather than pretending the half day is not there.

A half day is small, but a calendar cannot keep discarding it. The larger seasonal problem is different: twelve lunar months are about eleven days short of a solar year, so the Hebrew lunisolar calendar also adds a whole leap month in designated years. The family is delighted to discover that sometimes a residual gets promoted to an entire month. The accountant requests a larger ledger.

## The twenty-ninth of February

The solar year has its own remainder: about 0.2422 of a day beyond 365. The simple approximation adds a leap day every four years, which is slightly too much.

The Gregorian rule corrects that excess: century years normally skip the leap day unless divisible by four hundred. A correction of the correction, written into the calendar rather than left to memory.

The calendar is an accountant with excellent long-term memory and no tolerance for tiny unpaid fractions.

## In the repository

Mova's reconstruction work treats small residuals as measurements rather than cosmetic noise. Even when the remainder is tiny, it is counted at the level where the test is defined, because repeatedly rounding away losses can make the returned world drift from the one that went in.

The gate is the leap day in this note's metaphor: a scheduled correction, not a claim that every discrepancy can be fixed by adding one day.

## Two and nine

2 + 9 = 11. The eleventh note's two ones and no zero: counting on, in a single state, forever. The calendar never stops counting either. It only pays its remainder on time.

## Final note

Twenty-nine is a half day that will not round away, and a day added so that the year stays where it was.

Count the remainder.

Pay it back on time.
