# Expressions with their real meanings — the pragmatics lens

The pragmatics layer reads idioms, proverbs, phrasal and verb phrases, slang and register-marked words by their
real meaning, not literally. Knowledge is level 1: `global/data/expressions.tsv` (122 922 entries from the English
Wiktionary dump, `tools/wikt-idioms`, CC BY-SA): multiword entries (8 732 idioms, 1 433 proverbs, 2 712 phrases,
60 205 verb phrases, 55 phrasal verbs) and words or expressions with register labels, each label with the gloss of
the sense it marks.

- `global::expressions_in_sentence(forms, lemmas)` — non-overlapping matches, longest first, on lemmas ("kicked
  the bucket" → kick the bucket) and on forms ("spill the beans"); "one's" matches a possessive, "someone" one word.
- A register label switches a domain on only through the main sense: slang (slang, informal, colloquial, vulgar,
  derogatory, offensive), formal (formal, literary, archaic), humor (humorous, jocular, ironic, sarcastic), hidden
  meaning (euphemistic, figurative). Rare labelled senses do not ("old", "give up" have slang senses).
- `prag::lens::lens(words)` — readings (text, phrase, kind, meaning, register) and the domains; `prag::lens::means`
  — "what was meant" from the multiword readings. CLI: `prag lens <en model> "<text>"`.

Example: "Grandpa kicked the bucket, the poor old geezer." → kicked the bucket = to die (euphemistic, humorous);
geezer (informal, derogatory) → domains hidden-meaning, humor, slang.

Tried as features of the pragmatics classifiers (two slots: expression kind, domains): no clear gain on the silver
fields (cross-validated macro-F1 act 33.3 → 35.7, indirect 59.3 → 60.8, form 69.5 → 65.5 — within noise), so the
lens feeds "what was meant" and the domain switch, not the act classifier. The domains themselves (slang, formal,
humor modules) are the next step; the matrix's working mode already uses absurdity as the humor trigger.

## Domain modules (world domains, 2026-10-02)

`world domains "<text>" [--genre tale]` switches domains on with reasons; `world domains-eval <set.jsonl>`
measures them. Evidence: register labels on the main sense (slang, formal, humor, hidden meaning; regional
varieties as `variety:<country>`), idioms and proverbs of two or more content words, the formal register measured
on corpora (`global::register_z("legal")`: log-odds of 113 public-domain legal and official books against tales,
children's books and other books, Monroe et al. 2008; `world register-stats`), a register clash (formal language
about everyday things → mock-formal humor) and absurdity that survives the genre's domain layer (the fox speaking
is humor in the real world, literal in a tale).

Contrast sets generated by Opus through the Batches API: dev 200 sentences (thresholds chosen there),
test 200 independent sentences (65 literal controls each).

| domain | dev precision / recall | test precision / recall |
|---|---|---|
| slang | 58.3% / 41.2% | 59.3% / 47.1% |
| formal | 68.8% / 73.3% | 67.7% / 70.0% |
| humor | 30.0% / 14.3% | 28.9% / 17.7% |
| hidden meaning | 70.0% / 50.0% | 69.2% / 46.2% |
| literal sentences with a false alarm | 21.5% | 23.1% |

Before the statistics and filters: formal recall 33%, literal false alarms 35%. Humor is the weak one: jokes rest
on world knowledge and context, word statistics catch only mock-formal clashes and absurdity. Modern slang
("lowkey", "no cap", "ghosting") is mostly a secondary Wiktionary sense of common words; it needs time-sliced
corpus statistics (social media), not the dictionary. Corpus lexicons: `global/data/register-{legal,tale,children}.tsv`.
