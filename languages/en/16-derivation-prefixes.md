# 16. Derivation I: prefixes

A prefix is a bound element added before a base to make a new word: *un-happy, re-write, pre-war*. In English, prefixes almost never change the word class (*happy* and *unhappy* are both adjectives); they add a meaning of negation, reversal, degree, place, time, number or attitude. The few class-changing prefixes (*a-, be-, en-*) are old (§10). Prefixes come from two strata: native (*un-, mis-, over-, out-, fore-, be-, a-*) and Latin or Greek (*in-, dis-, re-, pre-, sub-, anti-, hyper-*), and the strata differ in what they attach to and how they sound (§1.2).

This chapter is organized by meaning, following Jespersen MEG VI chapters XXVI ("Negative and related prefixes"), XXVII ("Prepositional prefixes") and XXVIII ("Prefixes concluded"), and Sweet NEG I §§1569–1581 (native prefixes) and §§1623–1680 (foreign prefixes). Mova's seeds `en/seeds/morph/wf-negative-prefixes.md`, `wf-prefixes-neutral.md` and `wf-prefixes-class-changing.md` give the exact page references.

## 1. General properties

### 1.1 Class and meaning

| property | rule | examples |
|---|---|---|
| word class | kept | *write → rewrite* (V), *war → pre-war* (N → modifier), *confident → overconfident* (A), *pilot → co-pilot* (N) |
| head | the base is the head; the prefix modifies it | an *ex-president* is a kind of president (formerly) |
| stacking | prefixes stack, each scoping over what follows | *un-re-constructed, anti-dis-establishment-arian-ism, re-un-ify* |
| stress | usually secondary stress on the prefix, primary on the base; prefixes with strong meaning get "even stress" | *ˌunˈkind, ˌreˈwrite, ˌpre-ˈwar*; Sweet NEG I §919 lists *ˈunbeˈlief, ˈmisˈconduct, ˈex-ˈmanager, ˈsub-comˈmittee* as words where prefix and body are "balanced" by equal stress |

Whitney §101 already notes that prefixes do not change the part of speech; Reynolds 2026 §8.2.3 calls them "verb-changing prefixes" that "change the meaning of the verb without changing its lexical category", the most common being *re-, over-, un-, mis-, out-* (citing Biber et al. 1999).

### 1.2 Native vs Latinate

| | native and naturalized (*un-, re-, mis-, over-, out-, non-, pre-*) | Latinate, older (*in-, con-, sub-, ad-, ob-, dis-* in old loans) |
|---|---|---|
| bases | free words (*un-happy, re-paint*) | often bound roots (*in-ept, con-ceive, sub-mit*) |
| sound changes at the boundary | none: *unnatural* /ʌnˈnætʃ-/ with a long or double /n/ | assimilation: *in- → il-, im-, ir-*; *con- → col-, com-, cor-*; *ad- → ac-, af-, al-, ap-, as-, at-*; *sub- → suc-, suf-, sup-* |
| meaning | transparent | often opaque (*conceive, submit*) |
| productivity | high | low or none in the assimilated forms |

Ben Hedia 2019 measured the duration of the double consonant at the boundary in a corpus study and an experiment (ch. 6–7) and found a hierarchy (summed up at the end of §7.3 and in ch. 8): *un-* geminates (*unnamed, unnatural*) in both kinds of speech; negative and locative *in-* (*innumerable, input*) geminate about as much as *un-* in the corpus but less in the experiment; *dis-* (*dissatisfied*) geminates more weakly; the suffix *-ly* (*solely, really*) does not geminate at all: *un-* > *in-* > *dis-* > *-ly*. The results go against the stratal ("level 1 / level 2") prediction that Latinate *in-* should simply degeminate; she explains the gradient by how informative and how segmentable each affix is.

## 2. Negative prefixes

### 2.1 Overview

| prefix | attaches to | meaning | examples | productivity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *un-* | adjectives (any origin), participles, adverbs, some nouns | "not" (contrary or contradictory) | *unhappy, unkind, unable, unusual, unknown, unbelievable, unfortunately, unrest, untruth* | very high |
| *in-* (*il-, im-, ir-*) | Latinate adjectives | "not" | *inactive, incorrect, illegal, impossible, irregular, inedible* | low; mostly in existing loans |
| *dis-* | adjectives, nouns, verbs | "not; opposite" | *dishonest, disloyal, disadvantage, disagree, dislike, disobey* | moderate |
| *non-* | adjectives and nouns | "not, not a" (neutral, contradictory, classifying) | *non-smoker, non-fiction, non-existent, non-native, non-stop, non-profit* | high, esp. in technical use |
| *a-, an-* (Greek) | Greek-origin adjectives | "without" | *amoral, asymmetric, atypical, apolitical, anaerobic, anonymous* | moderate in learned words |
| *anti-* | nouns, adjectives | "against" (§5) | *antisocial, anti-war* | high |

Jespersen MEG VI 26.1–26.3 describes negative *un-*, *in-*, *a-/an-*, *n-* (*never, neither*), *no-* and *non-*; seed `en/seeds/morph/wf-negative-prefixes.md`.

### 2.2 *in-* and its assimilated forms

| before | form | examples |
|---|---|---|
| vowels and most consonants | *in-* | *inactive, incapable, indirect, infinite, insecure* |
| *l* | *il-* | *illegal, illogical, illiterate, illegible* |
| *b, m, p* | *im-* | *imbalance, immature, immoral, impossible, impatient, impolite* |
| *r* | *ir-* | *irregular, irrelevant, irresponsible, irreversible* |
| *gn* | *i-* | *ignoble, ignorant* (old) |

The same assimilation applies to the locative *in-/im-* "in, into" (*import, imprison, illuminate, irrigate*) and to *en-/em-* (*embrace, empower*). Jespersen MEG VI 26.2 records the assimilated forms and notes *invaluable* "priceless", *inflammable* "flammable" and *infamous* (/ˈɪnfəməs/) where *in-* is not negative or the meaning has shifted.

### 2.3 *un-* or *in-*?

The choice is lexical, with tendencies: *in-* goes with Latinate bases that came as whole words from Latin or French (*incapable, inactive*); *un-* goes with native bases (*unkind, unhappy*), with participles (*unknown, unexpected, unwilling*), with *-able* words even from Latin (*unacceptable, unavoidable*; *-ible* words prefer *in-*: *incredible, invisible*), and with most new formations. Doublets with different meanings: *unhuman/inhuman, unmoral/immoral, unartistic/inartistic, unreligious/irreligious*; *unable* but *inability* (Jespersen MEG VI 26.1).

### 2.4 Contrary and contradictory

*un-* and *in-* on gradable adjectives usually give a **contrary** (with a middle ground): *unhappy* is not just "not happy" but "sad"; one can be neither happy nor unhappy. *non-* gives a **contradictory** (no middle): *non-native, non-human*; it is neutral, while *un-* often evaluates: *un-American* (disloyal) vs *non-American* (not from America); *unscientific* (bad science) vs *non-scientific* (not science) (Jespersen MEG VI 26.3).

### 2.5 Negative prefixes and the grammar of negation

Affixal negation is **lexical**: it does not license negative polarity items as clausal *not* does (*\*He is unhappy at all*), and in UD the word gets no `Polarity=Neg` feature, which is reserved for *not, n't, no, nor* (UD `Polarity` documentation; seed `wf-negative-prefixes.md`). Reynolds 2026 §11.4.3 discusses affixal negation beside clausal and sub-clausal negation. The syntax of negation is in the chapters 20–38.

## 3. Reversative and privative prefixes

| prefix | attaches to | meaning | examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| *un-* (verbs) | verbs | reverse the action | *undo, untie, unlock, unpack, unfold, unwrap, unzip, uninstall* |
| *un-* (privative) | nouns → verbs | remove X | *unmask, unhorse, unearth, unseat* |
| *de-* | verbs, nouns | reverse; remove | *decode, defrost, deactivate, decentralize; debone, deforest, de-ice, debug* |
| *dis-* | verbs | reverse | *disconnect, disarm, disappear, disembark, disinfect, disown* |

The reversative *un-* of verbs (Old English *ond-*) is a different prefix from the negative *un-* of adjectives (Old English *un-*); with participles both are possible, so *unlocked* is ambiguous: "not locked" (negative *un-* + *locked*) or "having been unlocked" (participle of *unlock*). Jespersen MEG VI 26.4 calls the verbal one "privative *un-*"; Sweet NEG I §1580 treats *un-* as a living native prefix.

## 4. Prefixes of degree, size and evaluation

| prefix | meaning | examples |
|---|---|---|
| *over-* | too much; above | *overeat, overcook, overconfident, overpopulated, overcoat* |
| *under-* | too little; below | *underestimate, underpaid, underdeveloped, undercover* |
| *out-* | surpass (with transitive verbs); outside | *outrun, outlive, outnumber, outgrow; outbuilding, outpatient* |
| *super-* | above; very | *supermarket, superstar, superhuman, supernatural* |
| *hyper-* | excessively (Greek) | *hyperactive, hypersensitive, hypertext* |
| *ultra-* | beyond, extremely | *ultraviolet, ultra-modern* |
| *sub-* | below; less than | *subway, substandard, subtitle, subconscious* |
| *arch-* | chief; extreme | *archbishop, archenemy, arch-rival* |
| *mis-* | wrongly, badly | *misunderstand, misspell, mislead, misbehave, misfortune* |
| *mal-* | badly | *malfunction, malnutrition, maltreat, malpractice* |
| *pseudo-* | false | *pseudoscience, pseudonym* |
| *mini-, micro-* | small | *minibus, miniskirt; microwave, microchip* |
| *mega-* | big; million | *megastore, megabyte* |
| *co-* | joint | *co-author, co-pilot, coexist, cooperate* |
| *vice-* | deputy | *vice-president, vice-chancellor* |

*Out-* is unusual: added to an intransitive verb it makes a transitive one (*She outran him*: compare *\*She ran him*). Jespersen MEG VI 27.2 (*super-, sur-, hyper-*), 27.7 (*sub-, hypo-, infra-*), 26.6 (*mis-, mal-*), 28.6–28.8 (*pseudo-, auto-, vice-, arch-*).

## 7. Prefixes of time and order

| prefix | meaning | examples |
|---|---|---|
| *pre-* | before | *pre-war, prehistoric, preview, prepay, preschool* |
| *post-* | after | *post-war, postgraduate, postpone, post-modern* |
| *ante-* | before | *antenatal, anteroom* |
| *fore-* | before, in advance | *foresee, forecast, foretell, forewarn* |
| *ex-* | former | *ex-wife, ex-president, ex-smoker* (always hyphenated) |
| *re-* | again; back | *rewrite, rebuild, reconsider, replay, reopen; repay, return* |
| *neo-* | new | *neo-Nazi, neoclassical, neologism* |
| *proto-* | first | *prototype, proto-Germanic* |
| *retro-* | backward | *retrospective, retro-rocket* |

***Re-*** is the most productive verbal prefix; it attaches to verbs whose action can be repeated or reversed (*rewrite, reload, retweet*), seldom to stative verbs (*\*reknow*). Its pronunciation separates two layers: stressed, transparent /ˌriː-/ "again" (*re-cover* "cover again", *re-form, re-sign, re-count, re-press*) vs unstressed, opaque /rɪ-/ in old loans (*recover, reform, resign, recount, repress*). Writing distinguishes them with a hyphen when they would be homographs (Sweet NEG I §1668; Jespersen MEG VI 28.2; seed `wf-prefixes-neutral.md`).

## 8. Number prefixes

| prefix | meaning | examples |
|---|---|---|
| *uni-, mono-* | one | *unicycle, uniform, monorail, monotone, monolingual* |
| *bi-, di-* | two | *bicycle, bilingual, biannual* (twice a year), *biennial* (every two years), *dioxide* |
| *tri-* | three | *triangle, tricycle, trilingual* |
| *quad(ri)-, tetra-* | four | *quadruple, quadrilateral, tetrahedron* |
| *penta-, hexa-, octo-, deca-* | five, six, eight, ten | *pentagon, hexagon, octopus, decade* |
| *multi-, poly-* | many | *multinational, multimedia, polygon, polytechnic* |
| *semi-, hemi-, demi-* | half | *semicircle, semifinal, hemisphere, demigod* |
| *pan-* | all | *pan-American, pandemic* |

*Bi-* with time words is ambiguous (*bimonthly* "twice a month" or "every two months"). Jespersen MEG VI 28.3–28.5 (*demi-, semi-, hemi-, uni-, mono-, bi-, poly-, pan-*).

## 9. Prefixes in neoclassical and technical words

Greek and Latin combining forms (*auto-, bio-, geo-, tele-, photo-, psycho-, electro-, thermo-*) look like prefixes but are better analysed as bound stems that combine with other bound stems (*tele-phone, bio-logy*): they are treated with compounding in [18-compounding-conversion-minor.md](18-compounding-conversion-minor.md) §8. Recent clipped forms act like prefixes: *e-* (*e-mail, e-book*), *cyber-* (*cybersecurity*), *eco-* (*eco-friendly*), *nano-* (*nanotech*).

## 10. Class-changing prefixes

| prefix | from | to | meaning | examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *a-* | nouns, verbs | predicative adjectives, adverbs | "in, on, in a state of" (< *on*) | *asleep, afloat, alive, ablaze, ahead, aboard, ashore, awake, alike, aloud* |
| *be-* | nouns, adjectives, verbs | verbs; *be-…-ed* adjectives | "make, treat as; all over" | *befriend, belittle, becalm, bewitch, bemoan, besiege*; *bespectacled, bewigged, bejewelled* |
| *en-, em-* | adjectives, nouns | verbs | "make, put into" | *enlarge, enrich, enable, endanger, enslave, empower, embitter, entitle*; with *-en* suffix: *enlighten, embolden, enliven* |

The *a-* adjectives are predicative only (*the child is asleep*, \**an asleep child*) and have no *-ly* adverbs; *alive* vs attributive *live* (*a live broadcast*) (Jespersen MEG VI 27.3, 22.9; seed `en/seeds/nominal/a-adjectives.md`). *Be-* is "the only living old verbal prefix" by Sweet's account (NEG I §1572); *en-* was borrowed from French (§1643, §1653) (seed `en/seeds/morph/wf-prefixes-class-changing.md`).

## 11. Spelling: hyphen or not

Prefixed words are written solid by default (*rewrite, unhappy, prewar* in AmE), with a hyphen:

- before a capital or a number: *un-American, pre-1990, mid-July, post-9/11*;
- when the prefix ends and the base begins with the same vowel, especially in BrE: *re-enter, co-operate, pre-empt, anti-inflammatory* (AmE often *reenter, cooperate, preempt*);
- to avoid a homograph: *re-cover/recover, re-form/reform, re-sign/resign, co-op/coop*;
- with *ex-* "former", *self-*, *all-*, *quasi-*: *ex-wife, self-respect, all-powerful*;
- with prefixed compounds and phrases: *pre-Second World War, non-English-speaking*.

A prefix detached by coordination is written with a hanging hyphen: *pre- and post-war*, *over- and underestimation*; EWT tags such a detached prefix AFX ([10-word-classes.md](10-word-classes.md) §3).

## 12. Recognizing prefixes is harder than it looks

A word that begins with the letters of a prefix is often not prefixed. A naive count over Mova's lexicon (lemmas from UD and AGID in `en/data/lemmas.tsv`, flagged as known; a word counts if it starts with the prefix and the rest, at least four letters, is also a known lemma; counted 2026-10-17) finds:

| prefix | naive count | true examples | false analyses among the first found |
|---|---|---|---|
| *re-* | 591 | *reinsurance* | *re-fuse, re-lent, re-aper* |
| *un-* | 413 | *unclean, unapproachability, unrelatedness* | — |
| *in-* | 378 | *inauspiciousness* | *in-state* (verb *instate*), *in-habitation* (not negative) |
| *de-* | 287 | *dehumanisation, descale* | *de-test* |
| *dis-* | 247 | *disquiet* | *dis-solve, dis-grace* (historically prefixed, now opaque) |
| *over-* | 164 | *overcultivate, overcloud* | — |
| *non-* | 119 | *noncombatant* | *non-sense* (opaque) |
| *mis-* | 112 | *misplace, misapply, mishandle* | — |
| *pre-* | 110 | *preorder, precondition* | *pre-vent* (opaque) |
| *co-* | 83 | *coinsurance* | *co-lour, co-gently* |
| *ex-* | 66 | *exwife* | *ex-tent, ex-tension* |
| *ir-* | 25 | *irreversibility* | *ir-eland, ir-onside* |

Native, transparent prefixes (*un-, over-, mis-, out-*) give few false analyses; Latinate ones (*re-, de-, dis-, ex-, co-*) give many, because English borrowed both the prefixed Latin words and, separately, many of their "bases". A morphological analyser needs a lexicon of real derivations, not string matching.

## 13. How Mova handles prefixes

- **No segmentation.** Mova treats every prefixed word as its own lemma (*unhappy*, *rewrite*), as UD does. The lemmatizer strips only inflection; it never removes a prefix.
- **Unknown prefixed words** get their class from the tagger's suffix and shape features; since prefixes rarely change class, the suffix of the base is what matters (*unreconstructed* is tagged as a participle or adjective because of *-ed*).
- **Negation by prefix** is not marked in FEATS (UD rule); Mova's pragmatics and world layers would have to recover it from the lemma, which they currently do not do systematically.
- **Detached prefixes** (*pre- and post-war*) are tokenized as separate tokens and tagged AFX, as in EWT.
- **Resources for later work**: CatVar (`$MOVA_DATA/raw/en-catvar`, Open Software License 1.1) links derivational families across classes, mostly through suffixes; MorphoLex-en (`$MOVA_DATA/raw/en-morpholex`, CC BY-NC-SA 4.0, so for tests only) segments about 70 000 words into prefixes, roots and suffixes and could serve as a gold standard for a prefix analyser.
