# 00. Introduction: what English is, and how this manual writes it

## 1. The language in one paragraph

English is a West Germanic language. Its closest relatives are Frisian, Dutch, Low German and German; its grammar and its most frequent words are Germanic, but most of its dictionary vocabulary comes from French and Latin. It is written in the Latin alphabet with a spelling that was fixed, in the main, before the sound changes of the 15th–17th centuries had finished, so letters and sounds match only loosely. Its inflection is small: eight suffixes in all (plural *-s*, genitive *'s*, third person singular *-s*, past *-ed*, past participle *-ed/-en*, *-ing*, comparative *-er*, superlative *-est*), plus a set of irregular forms that are old and frequent. Grammatical relations are carried mostly by word order and by function words: auxiliaries, prepositions, articles. There is no one standard pronunciation, but there is a written standard, shared across nations with small differences in spelling and vocabulary.

## 2. Family and history in brief

| period | dates (conventional) | what changed | where it shows today |
|---|---|---|---|
| Old English | c. 450–1150 | Anglo-Saxon settlement; rich inflection (four cases, three genders, strong and weak declensions); Scandinavian contact from the 9th century | irregular plurals (*feet, mice, oxen*), strong verbs (*sing–sang–sung*), Scandinavian *they, them, their, egg, sky, take* |
| Middle English | c. 1150–1500 | after the Norman Conquest French becomes the language of the court and law; unstressed endings reduce to /ə/ and drop; grammatical gender disappears; a London-based written standard rises | thousands of French loans; the plural *-s* and genitive *'s* spread to nearly all nouns |
| Early Modern English | c. 1500–1700 | the printing press, the Great Vowel Shift, mass borrowing from Latin and Greek, the King James Bible (1611) and Shakespeare; *do*-support develops; *thou* gives way to *you* | the mismatch of spelling and vowel sound (*name, time, house*); learned words (*describe, encyclopedia*); the *-eth/-est* forms of [09](09-early-modern-and-archaic.md) |
| Late Modern English | c. 1700–1945 | dictionaries and prescriptive grammars; the progressive and the semi-modals spread; American English becomes a standard of its own | national spelling standards ([06](06-spelling-rules.md)); the vocabulary of science and empire |
| Present-day English | since c. 1945 | English becomes a global language; new national standards (Australia, India, Nigeria, Singapore…); computer-mediated writing | variation in [08](08-national-varieties.md) |

The period boundaries follow Hejná & Walkden 2022 (chapters 2–5), who also date Old English from the 5th-century settlement; Sweet NEG I §§605–646 gives the same outline in older terms (Old, Middle and Modern English; "Present English strata"). The details of the sound changes are in [02-vowels.md](02-vowels.md) §7, those of the inflection in the chapters on each word class.

Reynolds 2026 (§8.2.3) counts the inflectional suffixes the same way: possessive *'s*, verbal *-s*, *-ed*, *-ing*, plural *-s*, comparative *-er*, superlative *-est* (the past participle is the *-ed* form for regular verbs). Sweet NEG I §§74–81 discusses inflection by addition and by internal change, and the five ways English shows the relations of words (word order, form words, inflection, intonation, stress).

## 4. Standard English and its varieties

There is no academy and no legal standard. **Standard English** is the variety of grammar and vocabulary used in edited print, taught in schools and shared, with small differences, across the English-speaking nations. It is a written standard first: it can be spoken in any accent. Reynolds 2026 §2.2 argues for "Standard English(es)" in the plural: a British and an American standard at least, and several national ones, which agree on almost all grammar.

Two reference accents are used in this manual because almost all descriptions and dictionaries use them:

- **SSBE** (Standard Southern British English), the modern successor of **RP** (Received Pronunciation), the accent of educated speakers in the south of England; non-rhotic (no /r/ before a consonant or pause).
- **GA** (General American), the accent of most of the United States without marked regional features; rhotic.

The other national varieties (Scottish, Irish, Canadian, Australian, New Zealand, South African, Indian, Nigerian and Ghanaian, Singaporean English, and others) are described in [08-national-varieties.md](08-national-varieties.md). Hejná & Walkden 2022 §2.1 surveys them (Britain and Ireland, North America, Australia and New Zealand, India and South Africa).

**Mova's choice.** Mova reads every variety and writes Standard English. Its training treebanks are mostly American (EWT and ESLSpok; GUM, mixed genres, CC BY-NC-SA, enters only through Mova's dialect experiments, `en/README.md`), its school lessons are written in a neutral standard with deliberate slang and regional words ([docs/school.md](../../docs/school.md)), and its book lessons draw on British and American 19th-century tales. Spelling variants (*colour/color*) are lemmatized to the variant the lexicon knows; see [06-spelling-rules.md](06-spelling-rules.md) §9.

## 5. Where the vocabulary comes from

| stratum | typical words | typical properties |
|---|---|---|
| native (Old English) | *the, and, be, have, go, man, child, house, eat, good, foot, mother* | short, frequent, irregular forms, stress on the root, prefixes *be-, for-, un-*, suffixes *-ness, -ful, -dom, -ship* |
| Scandinavian (8th–11th c.) | *they, them, their, take, get, give, egg, sky, skin, sister, law, ill* | indistinguishable in behaviour from native words |
| French (from 1066; most in 1250–1400) | *people, city, beef, pork, judge, court, very, change, chair, fruit, beauty* | stress later moved to the first syllable in many (*cíty*), some kept final stress (*police*) |
| Latin (all periods, most in 1500–1700) | *describe, educate, populate, adjacent, focus, data, item* | Latinate affixes (*-ion, -ity, in-, re-*), stress by the Latin rule ([03](03-syllable-stress-intonation.md) §4) |
| Greek (mostly through Latin; learned coinages) | *phone, theory, democracy, psychology, telephone* | neoclassical compounding ([18](18-compounding-conversion-minor.md) §8) |
| other languages | *algebra* (Arabic), *chocolate* (Nahuatl via Spanish), *shampoo* (Hindi), *tea* (Chinese), *kindergarten* (German), *tsunami* (Japanese) | usually fully assimilated in spelling and sound |

Sweet NEG I §§605–646 traces the strata (Latin, Celtic, Scandinavian, French influence; the influence of other languages in Modern English; "present English strata"). Jespersen's *Growth and Structure of the English Language* (1905; library edition 1912) devotes chapters to the Scandinavian, French and Latin elements. Hejná & Walkden 2022 §§4.5.2–4.5.3 and §5.5.1 treat colonial, Latin, Greek and French borrowing.

Two general facts matter for a parser:

1. **The most frequent words are almost all native.** Of the 40 most frequent past-tense forms in Mova's lexicon counts (UD EWT + ESLSpok train, `en/data/forms.tsv`), 23 are irregular, all native verbs (*was, had, were, did, said, got, took, went, came, told, made, found, gave, thought, left, felt, saw, knew, brought, put, began, spent, sent*). Irregularity lives where frequency is high ([14-irregular-verbs.md](14-irregular-verbs.md) §1).
2. **Latinate words come in families** (*decide–decision–decisive*, *produce–product–production–productive*) linked by sound changes the spelling partly hides; [17-derivation-suffixes.md](17-derivation-suffixes.md) lists them.

## 6. The units of this description

- **Phoneme**: a sound that keeps words apart in a variety (*pat/bat*); its **allophones** are the ways it is pronounced in different positions ([01](01-consonants.md)).
- **Grapheme**: a letter or letter group that spells one phoneme (⟨sh⟩ = /ʃ/) ([05](05-writing-system.md)).
- **Morpheme**: the smallest unit with a meaning or grammatical function (*un-kind-ness*). A morpheme can have several **allomorphs**: plural /s/, /z/, /ɪz/ ([04](04-connected-speech.md) §6).
- **Word form** (shape, type) vs **lexeme** (lemma): *went* is a form of the lexeme GO. Mova's lemmatizer maps forms to lemmas ([13](13-verbs.md) §10).
- **Inflection** makes forms of one lexeme; **derivation** and **compounding** make new lexemes ([16](16-derivation-prefixes.md)–[18](18-compounding-conversion-minor.md)).
- **Multiword expression**: a unit of the lexicon made of several words (*give up, by and large, kick the bucket*) ([19](19-lexicon-mwe-register.md)).

Reynolds 2026 §8.2.4 distinguishes word families (*wide, widen, width*), lemmas (*widen, widens, widened, widening*), shapes and tokens; this manual follows that use.

## 7. Notation

### 7.1 Phonetic symbols

Consonants: /p b t d k ɡ f v θ ð s z ʃ ʒ h tʃ dʒ m n ŋ l r j w/. The symbol /r/ is used for the English approximant, phonetically [ɹ]. Vowels are listed with their keywords in [02-vowels.md](02-vowels.md) §2; the transcription is the one now usual in British dictionaries for SSBE, with General American values given beside it. Stress: /ˈ/ before the primary stressed syllable, /ˌ/ before a secondary one: *ˌphotoˈgraphic*.

Mova's data uses two other sound notations:

- **ARPAbet** in CMUdict (`$MOVA_DATA/raw/en-cmudict/cmudict.dict`, BSD-2-Clause): `K AE1 T` = /ˈkæt/, digits mark stress (1 primary, 2 secondary, 0 none).
- **Britfone** (`$MOVA_DATA/raw/en-britfone`, MIT licence): British pronunciations in IPA.
