# 08. National varieties

English is the first language of most people in Britain, Ireland, the United States, Canada (outside Quebec), Australia and New Zealand, and of a large minority in South Africa; it is an official or institutional language in India, Pakistan, Nigeria, Ghana, Kenya, Singapore, the Philippines and dozens of other countries (Hejná & Walkden 2022 §2.1.2 lists, among others, Belize, Guyana, Nigeria, Papua New Guinea, Eswatini, Tanzania, Zambia). The varieties share one written grammar almost entirely; they differ most in **pronunciation**, then in **vocabulary**, and least in **spelling** and **grammar**. This chapter lists the differences that matter to a reader and to a parser. The history of each variety is outlined by Hejná & Walkden 2022 §§2.1.2–2.1.5 (Britain and Ireland; North America from 1607; Australia from 1788 and New Zealand from 1840; India from 1602 and South Africa from 1795).

Two groups are usually distinguished:

- **Inner-circle** (native) varieties: British, Irish, American, Canadian, Australian, New Zealand, South African (white and coloured communities) English.
- **Outer-circle** (institutional, second-language) varieties: Indian, Pakistani, Nigerian, Ghanaian, Kenyan, Singaporean, Malaysian, Philippine English, among others. They have their own norms of pronunciation and vocabulary and some characteristic grammar, while their written standard follows British (or, in the Philippines, American) usage.

Reynolds 2026 §2.2 ("Standard English(es)") argues that these are several standards, not deviations from one.

### 2.2 Spelling

See [06-spelling-rules.md](06-spelling-rules.md) §9: *-our/-or, -re/-er, -ise/-ize, -ll-/-l-, -ogue/-og, ae/e, -ence/-ense* and individual words. VarCon counts 13 559 American–British pairs of word forms, two-thirds of them *-ise/-ize*.

### 2.4 Syntax in brief

These belong to syntax (chapters [20](20-syntax-overview.md)–[29](29-ellipsis-word-order-information-structure.md)) and are only listed here: the present perfect is used more in BrE (*I've just eaten* vs AmE also *I just ate*); BrE *at the weekend*, *in hospital*, *at university*, *different to/from* vs AmE *on the weekend*, *in the hospital*, *at the university / in college*, *different from/than*; AmE *Go see*, *Come get* (two bare verbs); BrE tag questions are more frequent; BrE *I'd like you to…* vs AmE *I'd like for you to…* (regional).

## 3. Scotland

Scottish English is the standard English of Scotland; **Scots**, a sister of English descended from Northern Middle English, is spoken beside it and colours it.

- **Sounds**: rhotic, often with a tap [ɾ]; /x/ in *loch, dreich*; /ʍ/ in *which, white*; no FOOT–GOOSE contrast (*full = fool* /ʉ/), no TRAP–PALM contrast (*Sam = psalm*), no LOT–THOUGHT contrast (*cot = caught*); vowel length set by the **Scottish vowel length rule** (long before /r v ð z/ and morpheme boundaries: *agreed* long vs *greed* short); FACE and GOAT are monophthongs [e o].
- **Grammar**: *wee* "small"; negative *-nae, no* (*canna, didnae, I'm no going*); *outwith* "outside"; *how?* "why?"; *needs washed* (participle after *need*, also in parts of the US); *yous* plural.
- **Words**: *aye* "yes", *bairn* "child", *ken* "know", *kirk* "church", *loch, burn* "stream", *glen, dreich* "dreary", *messages* "shopping", *close* "passage".

Jamieson's *Etymological Dictionary of the Scottish Language* (1808–) is downloaded as `$MOVA_DATA/raw/en-jamieson-scottish`; Wright's *English Dialect Grammar* (1905) covers Scotland, Ireland and the English counties.

## 4. Ireland

- **Sounds**: rhotic; clear /l/; dental stops for /θ ð/ (*thin* [t̪ɪn], *this* [d̪ɪs]): *tree = three* for some speakers; no FOOT–STRUT split in some rural speech; /hw/ kept; *film* [ˈfɪləm] with an epenthetic vowel.
- **Grammar** (many features from Irish Gaelic): the ***after*-perfect** (*I'm after eating* = I have just eaten); habitual *do be* (*he does be tired*); ***ye*** and ***youse*** as plural *you*; ***it's*-clefts** for emphasis (*It's tired you are*); *amn't I?*; *let you*; *sure* as a discourse marker; *himself* "the man of the house".
- **Words**: *craic* "fun, news", *grand* "fine", *press* "cupboard", *give out* "scold", *yoke* "thing", *gas* "funny".

## 5. Canada

Canadian English sits between British and American: American pronunciation and most vocabulary, British spelling of *-our* and *-re* with American *-ize* ([06](06-spelling-rules.md) §9.3), a few British words (*cheque, tap, washroom* for a public toilet).

- **Canadian raising** of /aɪ aʊ/ before voiceless consonants: *about* [əˈbʌʊt], *write* [rʌɪt] ≠ *ride* [raɪd] ([02-vowels.md](02-vowels.md) §6; Reynolds 2026 §7.4.2.3 describes it in his own Toronto speech).
- **Low-back merger**: *cot = caught*.
- **Canadian Shift**: lowering and retraction of KIT, DRESS, TRAP among younger speakers.
- **Words**: *eh* (tag), *toque* "knit cap", *loonie, toonie* (coins), *washroom*, *chesterfield* (older) "sofa", *double-double* (coffee).

## 6. Australia

- **Sounds**: non-rhotic; raised and fronted short vowels; wide diphthongs (*day* [dæɪ], *high* [hɒɪ], *now* [næɔ]); flapping of /t/; the BATH split only partly (*dance, plant, chance* with /æ/ for most speakers; *bath, laugh, grass* with /ɐː/); HRT (uptalk) on statements.
- **Morphology**: very productive **diminutives in *-ie/-y* and *-o***: *barbie* (barbecue), *brekkie* (breakfast), *mozzie* (mosquito), *sunnies* (sunglasses), *arvo* (afternoon), *servo* (service station), *journo, ambo, rego* ([18](18-compounding-conversion-minor.md) §5.3).
- **Words**: *ute* (utility vehicle), *bottle-o*, *g'day*, *mate*, *thongs* (flip-flops), *bush* (countryside), *footy*, *fair dinkum*, *no worries*; Aboriginal loans *kangaroo, boomerang, koala, billabong*.

## 7. New Zealand

- **Sounds**: like Australian but with a **centralized KIT** [ə] (*fish and chips* heard as "fush and chups" by Australians) and raised DRESS and TRAP (*bed* ≈ "bid", *bad* ≈ "bed" to outsiders); the NEAR–SQUARE merger (*beer = bear*); rhotic in Southland.
- **Words**: Māori loans in everyday use: *kia ora* (greeting), *whānau* (family), *kai* (food), *haka, marae, pākehā* (New Zealander of European descent); *jandals* (flip-flops), *chilly bin* (cooler), *bach* (holiday house), *dairy* (corner shop), *sweet as* (fine).

## 8. South Africa

- **Sounds**: non-rhotic; the **KIT split** ([ɪ] next to velars and /h/, [ə] elsewhere: *kit* [kɪt] vs *bit* [bət]); raised TRAP and DRESS in some groups; Afrikaans influence in some speakers (/x/ in *gogga*).
- **Words**: from Afrikaans *braai* (barbecue), *bakkie* (pickup), *veld*, *robot* (traffic light), *just now* (in a while), *now now* (very soon), *lekker* (nice); from Bantu languages *indaba* (meeting), *ubuntu*.

## 9. India and South Asia

Indian English is an established second-language variety with its own norms; it is the variety of education, law and national business for a very large number of speakers.

- **Sounds**: retroflex [ʈ ɖ] for /t d/; dental [t̪ d̪] (aspirated [t̪ʰ]) for /θ ð/; /v/ and /w/ often one sound [ʋ]; FACE and GOAT as monophthongs [eː oː]; often rhotic; **syllable timing** and less vowel reduction, so stress differs (*deˈvelopment* with full vowels).
- **Grammar**: progressive with stative verbs (*I am knowing him for years*); *itself* and *only* as focus markers (*today itself, he is coming only*); invariant tag *isn't it?*; *prepone* "move earlier"; *do the needful*; *revert* "reply"; *passed out* "graduated".
- **Words**: *lakh* (100 000), *crore* (10 million), *cousin-brother*, *hill station*, *tiffin*, *godown* (warehouse), *timepass*; many loans into general English came through India: *bungalow, shampoo, pyjamas, jungle, thug, cot, verandah, loot, juggernaut*. Yule and Burnell's *Hobson-Jobson* (1886; public domain; `$MOVA_DATA/raw/en-hobson-jobson`) records the Anglo-Indian vocabulary of the 19th century.

## 10. West Africa (Nigeria, Ghana)

- **Sounds**: non-rhotic; a reduced vowel system (no contrast of KIT–FLEECE, FOOT–GOOSE, often TRAP–STRUT–PALM); syllable timing and tone-like pitch.
- **Grammar**: pluralization of mass nouns (*furnitures, equipments, advices*); invariant tag *isn't it?*; *severally* "several times"; *borrow* for *lend*.
- **Words**: *go-slow* (traffic jam), *chop* "eat; food", *dash* "tip, gift", *flash* "make a missed call", *sorry* used in sympathy.
- **Nigerian Pidgin** (*Naijá*) is a separate English-lexifier language and needs its own grammar.

## 11. Singapore and Malaysia

**Singapore Standard English** follows British norms with local vocabulary (*HDB flat*, *void deck*, *hawker centre*). **Singlish** (Colloquial Singapore English) is a contact variety with a different grammar: discourse particles *lah, leh, lor, meh, hor*; dropped subjects and copula (*This one very nice*); *can* as an answer (*Can or not? – Can.*); *already* as a perfective marker (*He eat already*); Hokkien, Malay and Tamil loans (*shiok* "great", *kiasu* "afraid of losing out", *makan* "eat").

## 12. Dialects within England and the United States

The traditional dialects of England (Wright's *English Dialect Grammar*, 1905, and the *English Dialect Dictionary*, 1898–1905, both public domain: `$MOVA_DATA/raw/en-wright-edd`) keep older grammar that standard English lost:

- **Pronouns**: *thee, tha* (Yorkshire, Lancashire) for singular *you* ([09](09-early-modern-and-archaic.md)); *us* for *me* (*give us a kiss*), *her* as subject in the South-West, *hisself, theirselves* (regular reflexives), *them* as a demonstrative (*them books*).
- **Verbs**: the **Northern Subject Rule**: *-s* in the third person plural after a full noun subject but not after *they* (*the birds sings* but *they sing*), described by Hejná & Walkden 2022 §2.3.1; ***was/were* levelling** (*we was, it weren't*); ***ain't*** for *isn't, hasn't*; zero third-person *-s* in East Anglia (*he go*), which Hejná & Walkden discuss in the same section.
- **Negation**: multiple negation (*I didn't see nothing*), general in non-standard English everywhere.

In the United States, the main dialect regions are the North (with the Northern Cities Shift, [02](02-vowels.md) §6), the Midland, the South (PRICE monophthongization *ride* [raːd], *y'all*, *fixing to* "about to", double modals *might could*), New England (non-rhotic in eastern areas), New York City, and the West (merged *cot–caught*). **African American English** has a systematic grammar of its own: habitual *be* (*she be working*), copula absence (*she working*), *done* perfective (*I done told you*), stressed *BIN* (remote past), zero third-person *-s*, *ain't* as general negative auxiliary, negative concord.

## 13. How Mova handles varieties

- **Training data.** Mova's tagger and parser are trained on UD English EWT (mostly American web text) and ESLSpok, plus the agreed school trees. American forms dominate its lexicon counts (*color* NN 13 tokens vs *colour* 0; *center* NN 9 vs *centre* 6; `en/data/forms.tsv`, counted 2026-10-17), but British forms are present through AGID and the counts ([06](06-spelling-rules.md) §11).
- **School lessons.** The narration is in a neutral standard; by design, half of the conversational lines use the everyday mix and half "hard slang and dialect of the speaker's generation and place": US generations and the words of every English-speaking country ([docs/school.md](../../docs/school.md), "The voice"). So the student meets regional and generational vocabulary as part of its training.
- **Domains by variety.** The pragmatics lens marks words whose Wiktionary sense carries a regional label as `variety:<country>` evidence ([docs/pragmatics.md](../../docs/pragmatics.md), "Domain modules"); the expressions table itself is CC BY-SA and internal.
- **Not yet**: no variety identifier, no mapping of British to American vocabulary or spelling, no treatment of Singlish or Pidgin grammar. VarCon (spelling) and Horwill/Bartlett (vocabulary, public domain or old) are downloaded for that work; the repo's `dialects/` folder is about annotation dialects (Mova's own dialect of UD, [docs/dialect.md](../../docs/dialect.md)), not about English varieties.
