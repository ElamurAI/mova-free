# 01. Consonants

English has **24 consonant phonemes** in nearly every variety. The varieties differ much less in their consonants than in their vowels; where they do differ, it is mostly in how a phoneme is pronounced in a given position (the allophones), not in the inventory. The main exceptions are /r/ (pronounced before a consonant in rhotic accents only), /hw/ (a separate phoneme in some Scottish, Irish and conservative American speech), /x/ (in Scottish *loch*), and the loss of /h/ or /θ ð/ in some urban accents.

In each pair the first is **fortis** (voiceless, articulated with more energy), the second **lenis** (voiced, or partly voiced). /w/ is labial-velar and is listed twice. The table follows the usual modern analysis; Reynolds 2026 §7.4.1 counts "roughly 24" phonemes for his Canadian English and classifies them by place, manner and voicing in the same way. Sweet NEG I §§692–711 gives the older phonetic description (form, place, the aspirate, "R in English"); Kruisinga I chapters III–IV gives the same for the southern British standard of the 1920s.

## 2. The phonemes one by one

### 2.1 Plosives /p b t d k ɡ/

- **Aspiration.** The fortis plosives /p t k/ are aspirated, [pʰ tʰ kʰ], at the start of a stressed syllable: *pin* [pʰɪn], *a*ˈ*ttack* [əˈtʰæk]. After /s/ in the same syllable they are unaspirated: *spin, stop, skin* [spɪn, stɒp, skɪn]. Reynolds 2026 §7.5 illustrates the point with a candle flame, which moves for *top* and not for *stop*; Kruisinga I (text-1 pp. 34–35) describes aspiration as breath between the stop and the vowel. Aspiration is the main cue to the fortis–lenis contrast at the start of a word; /b d ɡ/ are often voiceless there too ([b̥ d̥ ɡ̊]) and differ from /p t k/ by lacking the puff of breath.
- **Pre-fortis clipping.** A vowel, nasal or /l/ is shorter before a fortis consonant in the same syllable: *bet* has a shorter vowel than *bed*, *bent* than *bend*. At the end of a word this length difference, not voicing, is the main cue: *mat* [mæt] vs *mad* [mæːd] (Reynolds 2026 §7.6).
- **Unreleased stops.** Before another stop, and often at the end of an utterance, a plosive may have no audible release: *a̲ct* [ækˀt], *goo̲d boy* [ɡʊd̚ bɔɪ].
- **Glottal reinforcement and glottalling.** Before a consonant or at the end of a phrase, /p t k/ (most of all /t/) are often reinforced with a glottal closure, [ʔt]; in many accents /t/ is replaced by a glottal stop: *football* [ˈfʊʔbɔːl], *not now* [nɒʔ naʊ], and between vowels in London, many urban British accents and increasingly elsewhere: *butter* [ˈbʌʔə], *water* [ˈwɔːʔə]. Hejná & Walkden 2022 §2.2.1 describes glottalling as a regular sound change of present-day English, most frequent for /t/, and stigmatized in spite of its spread. In GA a glottal stop is usual before a syllabic /n/: *button* [ˈbʌʔn̩], *mountain* [ˈmaʊʔn̩].
- **Flapping (tapping).** See §3.3.
- **Velar fronting and backing.** /k ɡ/ are articulated further forward before front vowels (*key*) than before back ones (*cool*); this is never contrastive.

### 2.2 Affricates /tʃ dʒ/

/tʃ/ and /dʒ/ count as single phonemes: they occur at the start of syllables, where /t/ + /ʃ/ is otherwise impossible (*chip*, but no \**tship*), and they behave as one unit in rhythm. Across a word boundary *t + sh* is not the affricate: *white shoes* [waɪt ʃuːz] vs *why choose* [waɪ tʃuːz]. Many /tʃ dʒ/ come from **yod coalescence**, the merging of /t, d/ with a following /j/: *nature* /ˈneɪtʃə/ (older /ˈneɪtjʊə/), *educate* /ˈedʒʊkeɪt/, and in casual speech across words: *don't you* [ˈdəʊntʃu], *did you* [ˈdɪdʒu].

### 2.3 Fricatives /f v θ ð s z ʃ ʒ h/

- **/θ ð/** are dental (tongue tip at or between the teeth). They are rare in the world's languages and are the commonest source of foreign accent in English: speakers replace them with [t d], [s z] or [f v]. Reynolds 2026 §7.4.1.3 gives the substitution of /s/ or /t/ for /θ/ as the standard example. Within English, **TH-fronting** ([f v] for /θ ð/: *think* [fɪŋk], *brother* [ˈbrʌvə]) is usual in London and spreading in British urban speech; **TH-stopping** ([t d] or dental [t̪ d̪]) is usual in Irish English, New York and Caribbean English and in many Englishes of Africa and Asia ([08-national-varieties.md](08-national-varieties.md)).
- **/ð/ is rare in the dictionary and very frequent in text.** In CMUdict only 0.07% of the phonemes in all headword transcriptions are /ð/ (541 occurrences; counted 2026-10-17, first pronunciation of each of 135 166 headwords), but /ð/ begins the most frequent words of the language: *the, this, that, these, those, they, them, their, there, then, than, though*. Initial /ð/ occurs almost only in function words.
- **/s z/** and **/ʃ ʒ/** are sibilants; they select the /ɪz/ form of the plural and the third person (*buses, wishes, garages*: [04-connected-speech.md](04-connected-speech.md) §6).
- **/ʒ/** is the rarest consonant (0.06% in CMUdict). It occurs medially (*measure, vision*) and in recent French loans initially and finally (*genre, beige, garage*); many speakers say *garage* with /dʒ/.
- **/h/** occurs only before a vowel (or /j/ in *huge*). **H-dropping** (*'ouse* for *house*) is usual in most urban accents of England and Wales but stigmatized; in the standard it is limited to the weak forms of *he, him, his, her, have, has, had* ([04](04-connected-speech.md) §2). Words of French origin once had silent ⟨h⟩, and four keep it: *hour, honest, honour, heir* (with derivatives); *herb* keeps it in GA. On *a/an* before /h/ see [12](12-pronouns-determiners-numerals.md) §8.2.
- **/x/**, the velar fricative, is a phoneme in Scottish English (*loch, Buchanan*) and occurs in some Welsh names and in German and Hebrew loans (*Bach, Chanukah*); elsewhere it is replaced by /k/.

### 2.4 Nasals /m n ŋ/

- /ŋ/ never begins a word. After it, a /ɡ/ is pronounced when the ⟨ng⟩ is inside a morpheme (*finger* /ˈfɪŋɡə/, *hunger, anger, English*) and also in the comparative and superlative (*longer* /ˈlɒŋɡə/, *strongest, younger*), but not at the end of a word or before a suffix added to a word that ends in /ŋ/ (*singer* /ˈsɪŋə/, *singing, longing*). Historically /ŋ/ was an allophone of /n/ before /k, ɡ/; the loss of final /ɡ/ made it a phoneme. In parts of the English Midlands and North-West the /ɡ/ is kept (*sing* [sɪŋɡ]).
- **"G-dropping"**: the ending *-ing* is pronounced /ɪn/ in casual speech in all varieties (*goin', nothin', somethin'*); spelled with an apostrophe in dialogue. Mova's lexicon has *somethin* as a noun form seen once in UD EWT train (`en/data/forms.tsv`).
- **Assimilation of place.** /n/ takes the place of a following consonant: *ten boys* [tem bɔɪz], *in case* [ɪŋ keɪs], *input* [ˈɪmpʊt]. Inside Latinate words this assimilation is fixed in spelling: *in-* → *im-possible, il-legal, ir-regular* ([16-derivation-prefixes.md](16-derivation-prefixes.md) §2.2).
- **Syllabic nasals**: after a plosive or fricative in an unstressed syllable, /ən/ is usually [n̩]: *button* [ˈbʌtn̩], *listen* [ˈlɪsn̩], *happen* [ˈhæpm̩] (with assimilation).

### 2.5 Laterals /l/

- **Clear and dark l.** In SSBE and many other accents /l/ is "clear" [l] before a vowel (*leaf, silly*) and "dark" (velarized) [ɫ] before a consonant or pause (*feel, milk*). GA, Australian and Scottish English use darkish [ɫ] in all positions; Irish and Welsh English often use clear [l] everywhere.
- **L-vocalization.** In London and South-East England, and increasingly in Australia and New Zealand, dark /l/ becomes a back vowel or glide [o, ʊ]: *milk* [mɪok], *bottle* [ˈbɒʔo].
- **Syllabic l**: *bottle* [ˈbɒtl̩], *little* [ˈlɪtl̩].
- **Silent l** before certain consonants: *walk, talk, chalk, calm, palm, half, could, would, should, folk, salmon*.

### 2.6 /r/ and rhoticity

The English /r/ is usually a postalveolar or bunched approximant [ɹ], often with lip rounding. Taps [ɾ] occur in Scottish English and as an allophone after /θ/ in conservative SSBE (*three*); trills [r] in older Scottish and in some Welsh and Indian Englishes; a uvular [ʁ] in the traditional Northumbrian burr.

**Rhoticity** is the main consonantal difference between accents:

| | rhotic | non-rhotic |
|---|---|---|
| where | GA, Canada, Scotland, Ireland, South-West England, parts of Lancashire, most of India | SSBE and most of England and Wales, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa, the traditional accents of the US South and Boston, New York (older), Singapore, Nigeria |
| *car* | [kɑːɹ] / [kɑɹ] | [kɑː] |
| *card* | [kɑɹd] | [kɑːd] |
| *car alarm* | [kɑɹ əˈlɑɹm] | [kɑːr əˈlɑːm] (linking /r/) |
| *law and order* | [lɔ ən ˈɔɹdɚ] | [lɔːr ənd ˈɔːdə] (intrusive /r/, [04](04-connected-speech.md) §4) |
| vowels | /ɚ/ in *letter*, r-coloured vowels | centring diphthongs and long vowels: *near* /nɪə/, *square* /skweə/ (SSBE: /skwɛː/) |

Non-rhoticity arose in south-eastern England in the 18th century and spread with the prestige of London speech; American and Scottish English kept the older pronunciation (Hejná & Walkden 2022 ch. 3 discusses the Late Modern English sound changes). Sweet NEG I §711 describes the southern English of his day as already non-rhotic, with /r/ "only before a vowel". In a non-rhotic accent the /r/ of the spelling appears when the next word begins with a vowel (*far away*), which the writing system never shows.

### 2.7 Approximants /j w/

- **/j/** occurs only before vowels. After a consonant it is found mostly before /uː/ or /ʊə/: *cute, music, few, pure, Europe*.
- **Yod-dropping.** After /t d n s z l θ/ in a stressed syllable, /j/ is lost in GA and much of eastern England: *tune* GA /tun/ vs SSBE /tjuːn/ or /tʃuːn/; *news* GA /nuz/ vs SSBE /njuːz/; after /r/ and /l/ + consonant it is lost everywhere (*rule, blue*); after /s/ and /l/ it is now rare in SSBE too (*suit, lute*). Reynolds 2026 §7.4 cites /ˈnu.kli.ɚ/ vs /ˈnju.kli.ɚ/ as a familiar variable of North American speech.
- **Yod coalescence** with /t d/ gives /tʃ dʒ/: *tune* /tʃuːn/, *duke* /dʒuːk/ (usual in younger SSBE; §2.2).
- **/w/** and **/hw/**: words spelled ⟨wh⟩ (*which, whine, where*) had /hw/; in most accents /hw/ has merged with /w/ (*which = witch*, the **wine–whine merger**), but /hw/ (often [ʍ]) is kept in Scotland, Ireland, and by some North American speakers (Reynolds 2026 §7.4 describes himself as one of them). The ⟨wh⟩ of *who, whom, whose, whole* is /h/.

## 3. Processes that change consonants

### 3.1 Voicing and devoicing

Lenis obstruents /b d ɡ v ð z ʒ dʒ/ are fully voiced only between voiced sounds; at the start or end of an utterance they are partly or wholly devoiced. Approximants /l r j w/ are devoiced after an aspirated plosive: *play* [pl̥eɪ], *try* [tɹ̥aɪ], *cue* [kj̊uː], *twin* [tw̥ɪn]. This is why the contrast in *plight–blight* is carried by the devoiced /l/.

### 3.2 Inflectional voicing alternations

Some nouns alternate a fortis final fricative with a lenis one in the plural or in a related verb. This is a fixed property of each word, not an active rule ([11-nouns.md](11-nouns.md) §2.3; [17-derivation-suffixes.md](17-derivation-suffixes.md) §9):

| fortis | lenis | examples |
|---|---|---|
| /f/ | /v/ | *wife–wives, leaf–leaves*; noun *belief* – verb *believe*, *half–halve*, *proof–prove*, *grief–grieve* |
| /θ/ | /ð/ | *path–paths* [pɑːðz] (in pronunciation only), *mouth–mouths*; noun *bath* – verb *bathe*, *breath–breathe*, *cloth–clothe*, *teeth–teethe* |
| /s/ | /z/ | *house* /haʊs/ – *houses* /ˈhaʊzɪz/; noun *house* – verb *house* /haʊz/, *use* /juːs/ – *use* /juːz/, *advice–advise*, *excuse* (noun /s/, verb /z/) |

Jespersen MEG VI chapter XII ("change of consonant without any addition of formative") collects these pairs; Sweet NEG I §§999–1001 treats the plural voicing as a property of old words with a long vowel or *l* before *f*.

### 3.3 Flapping (tapping)

In GA, Canadian, Australian, New Zealand and Irish English, /t/ and /d/ between a stressed vowel (or /r/) and an unstressed vowel are pronounced as a voiced tap [ɾ]: *water* [ˈwɑɾɚ], *city* [ˈsɪɾi], *ladder* [ˈlæɾɚ], *party* [ˈpɑɹɾi], and across words: *get it* [ˈɡɛɾɪt], *a lot of* [əˈlɑɾəv]. The contrast of *writer* and *rider*, *latter* and *ladder* is then lost or carried by vowel length (and, in Canada, by the quality of the diphthong: Canadian raising, [02-vowels.md](02-vowels.md) §6). After /n/ the /t/ may disappear: *winter* [ˈwɪnɚ] = *winner* in fast GA speech. SSBE uses [t] or a glottal stop here; the flap is an American shibboleth in British ears.

### 3.4 Elision

Consonants are often dropped in clusters, most of all /t/ and /d/ between two consonants: *next day* [neks deɪ], *handbag* [ˈhæmbæɡ], *last night* [lɑːs naɪt], *kept quiet* [kep ˈkwaɪət]. The past *-ed* and the negative *n't* are elided too (*I don't know* [aɪ dəʊ ˈnəʊ]), which makes morphology hard to hear. Details in [04-connected-speech.md](04-connected-speech.md) §3.

## 5. Consonants and grammar

Several grammatical endings are consonants whose form depends on the preceding sound:

| ending | after sibilant / after t, d | after other voiceless | after voiced |
|---|---|---|---|
| plural, genitive, 3rd sg *-s* | /ɪz/ *buses, judge's, washes* | /s/ *cats, Pat's, walks* | /z/ *dogs, Bob's, runs* |
| past, participle *-ed* | /ɪd/ *wanted, needed* | /t/ *walked, kissed* | /d/ *played, loved* |

The rule and its history are in [04-connected-speech.md](04-connected-speech.md) §6. Contractions are consonants too (*'s, 'd, 'll, 've, 'm, 're, n't*: [13-verbs.md](13-verbs.md) §9).

## 6. How Mova handles consonants

Mova is a text model and has **no phonological component** yet: it does not transcribe, and no part of the parser uses sound. Sound matters in three places, all open:

- **a / an** depends on the first sound of the next word (*an hour, a university, an MP*). The seed `en/seeds/nominal/a-an-sound.md` states the rule and notes that it needs pronunciation data (CMUdict) and word adjacency, which the v0 rule language lacks; so the choice is not checked.
- **The allomorphs of *-s* and *-ed*** are spelled the same, so the lemmatizer never needs them; a speech front end would ([04](04-connected-speech.md) §6).
- **Spelled pronunciations** of casual speech (*goin', gonna, wanna, dunno, nothin'*) reach the tokenizer as words. UD EWT splits *gonna* into *gon* + *na* (lemmas *go* + *to*) and Mova's lexicon has these entries from EWT train (`gon` VBG → *go*, 6 tokens; `na` TO → *to*, 10 tokens; counted 2026-10-17 in `en/data/forms.tsv`), but `en/src/tok.rs` has no rule that splits *gonna* or *wanna* itself; the tagger and parser see the whole word.
