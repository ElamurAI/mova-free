# The moods and the complex sentence

Greek has four moods and a particle, **ἄν**, that changes what they mean. The **subjunctive** looks to the future and to what is willed or expected. The **optative** is the mood of the remote: of wishes, of the merely possible, and, after a past verb, of reported speech. The optative is the first thing to go: it is common in Herodotus and Plato and almost gone in the New Testament. Reported statements are made with the accusative and infinitive, with a participle, or with ὅτι and a finite verb. The late language moves from the first to the last, as Latin moved from the accusative with the infinitive to *quod*. This chapter gives:
- the moods in main clauses;
- ἄν;
- purpose, result, cause, time and condition;
- indirect discourse;
- the change from classical to Koine, with counts.

## 1. The moods in main clauses

(smyth-1920 §§1759–1849)

| mood | in a main clause | example |
|---|---|---|
| indicative | statements and questions of fact; with ἄν the unreal (§6) | ἔγραψεν "he wrote" |
| subjunctive | exhortation (1st person): ἴωμεν "let us go"; deliberation: τί ποιῶ; "what am I to do?"; prohibition with μή + aorist: μὴ ποιήσῃς "don't do it" | |
| optative | a wish for the future (often with εἴθε, εἰ γάρ): εἴθε γένοιτο "may it happen"; with ἄν, the potential: γένοιτ᾽ ἄν "it could happen", λέγοιμ᾽ ἄν "I would say, I'd like to say" | |
| imperative | commands: λέγε "speak", μὴ λέγε "stop speaking" | |

The negative is οὐ with the indicative and the potential optative, μή with the subjunctive, the wish optative and the imperative ([chapter 12](12-prepositions-particles.md), §4).

## 2. The particle ἄν

ἄν has no translation of its own. It makes a statement contingent: on a condition, on circumstances, on an occasion (smyth-1920 §§1761–1798):
- with the **past indicative**, the unreal: ἔγραψεν ἄν "he would have written" (but did not);
- with the **optative**, the potential: γράφοι ἄν "he might write, he would write";
- with the **subjunctive** in subordinate clauses, the general or future condition: ἐὰν (= εἰ ἄν) γράφῃ "if he writes", ὅταν (= ὅτε ἄν) ἔλθῃ "whenever he comes", ὃς ἂν ἔλθῃ "whoever comes";
- with the **infinitive and participle**, the same values in indirect discourse.

ἄν is a postpositive word ([chapter 13](13-simple-clause.md), §6) and often stands early in the clause, far from its verb, or is repeated.

## 3. Purpose and object clauses: ἵνα, ὅπως, ὡς, μή

**Purpose** is ἵνα, ὅπως, ὡς "in order that" (negative ἵνα μή, or μή alone) with the subjunctive, or after a past verb the optative (smyth-1920 §§2193–2206):

> ἔρχομαι ἵνα ἴδω "I come to see"; ἦλθεν ἵνα ἴδοι "he came to see"

Greek also expresses purpose by the future participle ([chapter 10](10-nonfinite.md), §5), by τοῦ + infinitive and by a relative clause with the future indicative.

**Object clauses after verbs of effort** (ἐπιμελοῦμαι, σκοπῶ, πράττω): ὅπως + future indicative: ἐπιμελεῖται ὅπως καλῶς ἕξει "he takes care that it shall be well" (smyth-1920 §§2207–2220).

**Verbs of fearing**: μή + subjunctive (optative after a past verb) for what is feared; μὴ οὐ for what is feared not to happen: φοβοῦμαι μὴ ἔλθῃ "I fear that he may come" (smyth-1920 §§2221–2233). This μή is the Latin *nē* of fear ([la chapter 10](../la/10-subordination.md), §4).

## 4. Result: ὥστε

ὥστε "so that, so as" (smyth-1920 §§2249–2278):
- with the **infinitive**, the natural or possible result: οὕτως ἀνόητος ἦν ὥστε τοῦτο πιστεύειν "he was foolish enough to believe it";
- with a **finite verb**, the actual result as a fact: οὕτως ἀνόητος ἦν ὥστε τοῦτο ἐπίστευσεν "he was so foolish that he believed it".

## 5. Causal and temporal clauses

**Cause**: ὅτι, διότι, ἐπεί, ἐπειδή, ὡς "because, since" with the indicative (the optative after a past verb for a reported reason) (smyth-1920 §§2240–2248).

**Time** (smyth-1920 §§2383–2461):
- ὅτε, ἡνίκα "when"; ἐπεί, ἐπειδή "after, when"; ἕως, μέχρι "while, until"; πρίν "before, until";
- with the **indicative** for a definite past or present time: ἐπεὶ ἦλθεν "when he came";
- with **ἄν + subjunctive** for an indefinite future or general present: ἐπειδὰν ἔλθῃ "when(ever) he comes";
- with the **optative** (no ἄν) for repeated action in the past: ἐπεὶ ἔλθοι "whenever he came";
- **πρίν** takes the infinitive after an affirmative main clause ("before"), a finite verb after a negative one ("until").

## 6. Conditional sentences

(smyth-1920 §§2280–2368)

| type | protasis (if-clause) | apodosis | example |
|---|---|---|---|
| simple present or past | εἰ + indicative | indicative | εἰ τοῦτο λέγει, ἁμαρτάνει "if he says this, he is wrong" |
| future more vivid | ἐάν + subjunctive | future indicative | ἐὰν τοῦτο λέγῃ, ἁμαρτήσεται "if he says this, he will be wrong" |
| future less vivid | εἰ + optative | optative + ἄν | εἰ τοῦτο λέγοι, ἁμαρτάνοι ἄν "if he should say this, he would be wrong" |
| present unreal | εἰ + imperfect | imperfect + ἄν | εἰ τοῦτο ἔλεγεν, ἡμάρτανεν ἄν "if he were saying this, he would be wrong" |
| past unreal | εἰ + aorist | aorist + ἄν | εἰ τοῦτο εἶπεν, ἥμαρτεν ἄν "if he had said this, he would have been wrong" |
| present general | ἐάν + subjunctive | present indicative | ἐάν τις τοῦτο λέγῃ, ἁμαρτάνει "if anyone says this, he is (always) wrong" |
| past general | εἰ + optative | imperfect | εἴ τις τοῦτο λέγοι, ἡμάρτανεν "if anyone said this, he was (always) wrong" |

The negative of the protasis is μή, of the apodosis οὐ. The Latin system ([la chapter 10](../la/10-subordination.md), §8) has the same three main types; Greek has the general conditions as well.

## 7. Relative clauses with a conditional sense

A relative clause with an indefinite antecedent follows the conditional system: ὃς ἂν ἔλθῃ "whoever comes" = ἐάν τις ἔλθῃ; ὅστις ἔλθοι "whoever came (each time)" (smyth-1920 §§2553–2573). The negative is μή. These are the general relative clauses of laws and rules: ὃς ἂν κλέψῃ … "whoever steals …".

## 8. Indirect discourse

A statement is reported in three ways (smyth-1920 §§2574–2635):

| construction | after | example |
|---|---|---|
| **ὅτι** or **ὡς** + finite verb | λέγω, ἀγγέλλω, ἀποκρίνομαι, and verbs of knowing | λέγει ὅτι ὁ ἀνὴρ ἦλθεν "he says that the man came" |
| **accusative and infinitive** | φημί, νομίζω, οἴομαι, ἡγοῦμαι | φησὶ τὸν ἄνδρα ἐλθεῖν |
| **participle** | verbs of perceiving and knowing (ὁρῶ, ἀκούω, οἶδα, γιγνώσκω) | ὁρῶ τὸν ἄνδρα ἐλθόντα "I see that the man has come" |

**After a past verb**, the verb of a ὅτι clause may keep its mood and tense or change to the optative of the same tense: εἶπεν ὅτι ὁ ἀνὴρ ἔλθοι "he said that the man had come". The infinitive and participle keep the tense of the direct statement: an aorist infinitive in indirect discourse means a past event, not perfective aspect alone ([chapter 10](10-nonfinite.md), §2).

**Indirect questions** keep the interrogative or take the ὁ- forms of the correlatives (ὅστις, ὅπου, ὅπως: [chapter 6](06-pronouns.md), §8), with the same moods as ὅτι clauses.

## 9. From classical to Koine: the optative and ὅτι

Two changes mark the move from classical to Koine Greek: the loss of the optative, and the spread of ὅτι and ἵνα clauses at the expense of the infinitive. On our data (UD 2.18 training sets, counted 2026-10-22):

| text | treebank | finite verbs | subjunctive | optative | accusative + infinitive | ὅτι clauses | ἵνα clauses |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Herodotus, *Histories* | PROIEL (NC, counted only) | 7 196 | 4.0% | **3.9%** | **583** | 69 | 43 |
| the New Testament | PROIEL (NC, counted only) | 16 209 | 9.6% | **0.3%** | 398 | **875** | **561** |
| the Septuagint, Genesis and Ruth | PTNK | 2 348 | 5.3% | 2.0% | 23 | 93 | 16 |

The pattern of the Latin of the same centuries:
- in Herodotus the accusative with the infinitive outnumbers ὅτι clauses eight to one, and the optative is as common as the subjunctive;
- in the New Testament ὅτι clauses outnumber the infinitive two to one, the optative has almost gone, and ἵνα with the subjunctive spreads to every kind of complement (θέλω ἵνα "I want that …"), the ancestor of the Modern Greek να;
- the Vulgate turned in the same way, from the accusative with the infinitive to *quod* and *quia* ([la chapter 10](../la/10-subordination.md), §11).

The Septuagint keeps some optatives (wishes and blessings: γένοιτο, δῴη) and uses ὅτι for the Hebrew *kî* "that, because".

## 10. How Mova handles it

**In the UD trees.**
- A subordinate clause is `advcl`, `ccomp` or `acl:relcl`, with its conjunction as `mark`.
- The mood is `Mood=Sub`, `Mood=Opt` in the `FEATS`.
- ἄν is `ADV` or `PART` attached as `advmod` to its verb, which the annotators find even when ἄν stands far away.
- The accusative subject of an infinitive is an `nsubj` with `Case=Acc`.

**In Mova.** A Greek frames reader needs, in order:
1. **Mood and ἄν together** (§2): ἄν with a past indicative is unreal, with an optative potential, with a subjunctive general or future. The English reader's `irrealis` and `cond` marks hold all three, and the table of §6 maps the types onto them exactly.
2. **The reported content**, whatever its form, as a reported proposition with its speaker: ὅτι + finite verb, the accusative and infinitive, the participle after verbs of perceiving. The English reader does the same with *that* clauses.
3. **The tense of an infinitive in indirect discourse** read as time, not aspect (§8).
4. **The general relative and conditional clauses** (§7) read as rules: "whoever does X, Y", the form of law and of maxims, and a frame the world of a text can hold as a rule rather than an event.

The counts of §9 make the same stylometric signal as the word order of [chapter 13](13-simple-clause.md), §5: the optative and the infinitive against ὅτι tell classical from Koine without any model.
