# 2. Nouns: gender, number, case, and the five declensions

A Latin noun has a **gender** (masculine, feminine, neuter), a **number** (singular, plural) and, in a sentence, one of six **cases**. The case-endings change with the **declension**, the class the noun belongs to. This chapter gives the classes and their endings, the rules that sort nouns into them, and the irregular and borrowed nouns. What each case does in a sentence is chapter 7.

## 1. Gender

The three genders are not sex, except for persons and animals (Bennett 1918 §§13–15). A noun's gender is **natural** where it names a male or female being (*pater*, *māter*, *nauta* "sailor" masculine in spite of its first-declension ending) and otherwise **grammatical**, decided by the meaning class or by the ending:

| gender | by meaning | by ending |
|---|---|---|
| **masculine** | male beings; rivers, winds, months, mountains (*Tiberis*, *Aquilō*, *Aprīlis*, *Aetna*, an exception) | *-us, -er, -or, -ōs* (II, III); *-ō, -tor* (III); *-us* of IV |
| **feminine** | female beings; trees, islands, towns, countries (*pīnus*, *Rhodus*, *Aegyptus*); abstract nouns | *-a* (I); *-trīx, -tās, -tūdō, -iō, -x, -s* after a consonant, *-is, -ēs* (III); *-ēs* (V) |
| **neuter** | indeclinable words used as nouns (*nefās*); infinitives; letters of the alphabet | *-um* (II), *-ū* (IV), *-us* in *genus, tempus*; *-e, -al, -ar* (III) |

A neuter noun has the same nominative, accusative and vocative, and in the plural all three end in *-a* (Bennett 1918 §17, §20). This sameness is the first syncretism of Latin and recurs in the adjective and the pronoun (chapters 3–4).

Exceptions are numerous and are the classical teaching points: *agricola, nauta, poēta* masculine in the first declension; *humus, domus, fāgus* and the names of trees feminine in *-us*; *vulgus, pelagus, virus* neuter in *-us*; *diēs* and *rēs*: *diēs* masculine in the singular and feminine in the sense of a fixed day, *rēs* feminine. A **common gender** noun is masculine or feminine according to the person: *cīvis*, *parēns*, *dux*, *comes*.

## 2. Number

The plural of a noun is its singular-plural pair in the declension below. Several nouns lack one number (Bennett 1918 §§55–61):

- **plurale tantum**: *arma* "arms", *castra* "camp", *moenia* "city walls", *litterae* "letter, literature", *dīvitiae* "wealth", *angustiae* "a pass", *Athēnae* "Athens", *Kalendae*, *nūptiae*;
- **singulare tantum**: names of materials and collectives and of abstracts: *aurum*, *vīnum*, *iūstitia*, *senectūs*;
- **different meanings**: *castrum* "fort", *castra* "camp"; *aedēs* "temple", *aedēs* (pl.) "house"; *auxilium* "help", *auxilia* "auxiliary troops"; *fīnis* "end", *fīnēs* "boundaries, territory", as in the sentence *Helvetii … e finibus suis exeant* (Caes. *B. G.* 1.5).

## 3. The six cases and the stem

| case | abbreviation | the usual role (chapter 7) |
|---|---|---|
| nominative | nom. | subject; predicate noun |
| genitive | gen. | possessor, whole, quality, object of a noun |
| dative | dat. | recipient, interest, purpose |
| accusative | acc. | direct object; goal of motion; extent |
| vocative | voc. | address |
| ablative | abl. | source, means, manner, place, time, absolute |

A seventh case, the **locative** (place where), is left in small, frequent words (§9.4). The cases of the Indo-European parent that were merged in Latin are the ablative, which combines the old ablative (separation), instrumental (means) and locative (place) (Bennett 1918 §213); the dative's functions include some of the old locative as well.

The form of a noun is a **stem** plus an **ending**; the stem is found by dropping the ending of the genitive singular. The declensions are named for the stem vowel: **I** *ā*-stems, **II** *o*-stems, **III** consonant- and *i*-stems, **IV** *u*-stems, **V** *ē*-stems (Bennett 1918 §§18–54). A dictionary gives the nominative and the genitive singular and the gender (*rēx, rēgis, m.*), and that is the minimum a learner needs, since the nominative of the third declension does not show the stem.

## 4. First declension (ā-stems)

*rosa* f. "rose", stem *rosā-*; *nauta* m. "sailor":

| | sg. | pl. |
|---|---|---|
| nom. | rosa | rosae |
| gen. | rosae | rosārum |
| dat. | rosae | rosīs |
| acc. | rosam | rosās |
| voc. | rosa | rosae |
| abl. | rosā | rosīs |

The ending *-a* is short and *-ā* long. Almost all nouns are feminine; the masculine ones denote men (*nauta, poēta, agricola, scrība, Catilīna*). Special forms:

- **Archaic and fixed genitive in *-ās***: *pater familiās* "head of a household" (*familiās* for *familiae*).
- **Genitive plural in *-um*** in a few words, chiefly Greek (*Dardanidum*) and compounds in *-cola* (*caelicolum*); and *drachmum, amphorum*.
- **Dative/ablative plural *-ābus*** to avoid ambiguity with the second declension: *deābus* "to goddesses", *fīliābus* "to daughters" (Bennett 1918 §21).
- **Locative singular *-ae***: *Rōmae* "at Rome", *Athēnīs* with plural names (Cic. *Cat.* 1.9: *quos Romae relinqueres*, "whom you should leave behind at Rome").
- **Greek nouns** keep Greek endings in part: *Aenēās* m. (gen. *Aenēae*, dat. *Aenēae*, acc. *Aenēān* or *Aenēam*, voc. *Aenēā*, abl. *Aenēā*), *Anchīsēs, comētēs, epitomē* (Bennett 1918 §22). The Aeneid has *Aeneas* in the nominative in *Praecipue pius Aeneas nunc acris Oronti* (Verg. *Aen.* 1.220).

> **Caes. *B. G.* 1.1** · Latin Library
> Gallia est omnis divisa in partes tres, quarum unam incolunt Belgae, aliam Aquitani, tertiam qui ipsorum lingua Celtae, nostra Galli appellantur.
> "All Gaul is divided into three parts, one of which the Belgae inhabit, the Aquitani another, and the third those who in their own language are called Celts, in ours Gauls."

*Gallia* (nom. sg.), *partes* (acc. pl. of the third declension, *pars, partis*), *lingua* (abl. sg., "in their language", means or respect), *Belgae, Aquitani, Celtae, Galli* (nom. pl.: *-ae* of the first declension, *-ī* of the second) show the pattern.

## 5. Second declension (o-stems)

*dominus* m. "lord", *puer* m. "boy", *ager* m. "field", *bellum* n. "war":

| | sg. m. | sg. m. | sg. m. | sg. n. | pl. m. | pl. n. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| nom. | dominus | puer | ager | bellum | dominī | bella |
| gen. | dominī | puerī | agrī | bellī | dominōrum | bellōrum |
| dat. | dominō | puerō | agrō | bellō | dominīs | bellīs |
| acc. | dominum | puerum | agrum | bellum | dominōs | bella |
| voc. | domine | puer | ager | bellum | dominī | bella |
| abl. | dominō | puerō | agrō | bellō | dominīs | bellīs |

- Masculine and feminine nouns end in **-us** (and **-er, -ir**: *puer, ager, vir, gener*); neuters in **-um**. The nouns in *-er* either keep the *e* through the paradigm (*puer, puerī*, the old *-eros*), or lose it (*ager, agrī*).
- The **vocative** of *-us* nouns is **-e**: *domine, amice*; nouns in *-ius* have *-ī* (*fīlī, Vergilī, Gāī*) and the vocative of *deus* in classical Latin is lacking. The Vulgate writes *Deus* as vocative, from the Greek and Hebrew pattern: *Deus, Deus meus, respice in me* (Ps 21:2 Vulgate), "God, my God, look upon me."
- **Genitive singular of nouns in -ius and -ium**: *fīlī* for *fīliī*, *ingenī* for *ingeniī* (Bennett 1918 §25).
- **Genitive plural in -um** is frequent for coins, measures and weights (*nummum, sestertium*, *deum* for *deōrum*), and for *virum, deum* in verse.
- **Locative**: *humī* "on the ground", *domī* "at home", *Corinthī*, *Tarentī* (Bennett 1918 §232). *Domi* is seen in *gratia plurimum domi atque in reliqua Gallia* (Caes. *B. G.* 1.20), "who had the greatest influence at home and in the rest of Gaul."
- **Nominative plural of *deus*** may be *dī, deī* or *diī*; the dative and ablative plural are *dīs, deīs, diīs* (Bennett 1918 §26).
- **Neuters in -us**: *vulgus, pelagus* and *virus* are neuter though they end in *-us*; the nominative and accusative are alike (*vulgus*), and the plural is lacking.
- **Greek** nouns in *-os, -on* (*Delos, Ilion*) keep Greek endings in poetry.

The nominative and accusative of neuters are identical: *bellum* is "war" as subject and as object, and only the context separates the two. In *quibuscum continenter bellum gerunt* (Caes. *B. G.* 1.1), "with whom they continually wage war", *bellum* is the object.

## 6. Third declension

The third declension is the largest and the most varied. Its stems end in a consonant or in *-i-*, and the nominative singular often does not reveal the stem (*rēx* from *rēg-*, *nōmen* from *nōmin-*), hence the dictionary's genitive. Gender is decided by meaning or ending (Bennett 1918 §§43–46).

### 6.1 The endings

| | consonant stems m./f. | consonant stems n. | *i*-stems m./f. (*hostis*, *urbs*) | *i*-stems n. (*mare*) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| nom. sg. | (varies) | (varies) | hostis, urbs | mare |
| gen. sg. | -is | -is | -is | -is |
| dat. sg. | -ī | -ī | -ī | -ī |
| acc. sg. | -em | = nom. | -em | = nom. |
| voc. sg. | = nom. | = nom. | = nom. | = nom. |
| abl. sg. | -e | -e | -e (sometimes -ī) | -ī |
| nom./acc./voc. pl. | -ēs | -a | -ēs | -ia |
| gen. pl. | -um | -um | -ium | -ium |
| dat./abl. pl. | -ibus | -ibus | -ibus | -ibus |
| acc. pl. | -ēs (older -īs) | -a | -ēs, -īs | -ia |

### 6.2 Consonant stems

Examples (Bennett 1918 §§29–36):

| | rēx m. "king" | mīles m. "soldier" | lēx f. "law" | homō m. "man" | nōmen n. "name" | tempus n. "time" | pater m. | cōnsul m. |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| nom. sg. | rēx | mīles | lēx | homō | nōmen | tempus | pater | cōnsul |
| gen. sg. | rēgis | mīlitis | lēgis | hominis | nōminis | temporis | patris | cōnsulis |
| acc. sg. | rēgem | mīlitem | lēgem | hominem | nōmen | tempus | patrem | cōnsulem |
| abl. sg. | rēge | mīlite | lēge | homine | nōmine | tempore | patre | cōnsule |
| nom. pl. | rēgēs | mīlitēs | lēgēs | hominēs | nōmina | tempora | patrēs | cōnsulēs |
| gen. pl. | rēgum | mīlitum | lēgum | hominum | nōminum | temporum | patrum | cōnsulum |

The stem consonant gives the nominative its shape: a guttural + *s* gives *x* (*rēg-s* > *rēx*); a dental drops before *s* (*mīlit-s* > *mīles*, with change of vowel); *-o, -n* stems drop the *n* (*homo*, *hominis*); *-or, -os* stems show **rhotacism** (*honōs* > *honor*, *honōris*; chapter 1 §6). Neuters in *-us* have *-or-* in the oblique cases (*tempus, temporis*; *corpus, corporis*) or *-er-* (*genus, generis*; *vulnus, vulneris*).

*Tempora* and *mores* are consonant-stem plurals in the most quoted sentence in Latin: *O tempora, o mores!* (Cic. *Cat.* 1.2).

### 6.3 *I*-stems

A noun is an *i*-stem if it is **parisyllabic in *-is* or *-ēs*** (the genitive has as many syllables as the nominative: *hostis, hostis*; *nūbēs, nūbis*; *caedēs, caedis*), or if it is a **monosyllable ending in two consonants** (*urbs, urbis*; *mons, montis*; *nox, noctis*; *pars, partis*); and **neuters in *-e, -al, -ar*** (*mare, animal, calcar*). Their genitive plural is *-ium* and the neuter plural *-ia*; the ablative singular of neuters is in *-ī* (*marī, animālī*). In practice there is a mixed class: consonant-stems that took *-ium* (*pater* has *patrum*, but *cīvis* has *cīvium*, and *imber, linter, uter* have *-ium*). Hence the usual error of the learner: the genitive plural can be *-um* or *-ium*, and the genitive singular alone does not tell which. The accusative plural in *-īs* (*omnīs, urbīs*) is the older and Augustan spelling of *-ēs*.

> **Cic. *Cat.* 1.3** · Latin Library
> Catilinam orbem terrae caede atque incendiis vastare cupientem nos consules perferemus?
> "Shall we the consuls bear with Catiline, who is eager to lay waste the world with slaughter and fire?"

*Orbem* (acc. sg., *orbis*), *terrae* (gen. sg. I), *caede* (abl. sg., *caedēs* parisyllabic; *i*-stem but with ablative in *-e*), *incendiis* (abl. pl. II), *consules* (nom. pl., apposition).

| *sūs* c., *bōs* c. | *suis*, *bovis*; gen. pl. *boum*, dat./abl. pl. *bōbus, būbus* | |

## 7. Fourth declension (u-stems)

*manus* f. "hand", *exercitus* m. "army", *cornū* n. "horn":

| | sg. | pl. | sg. n. | pl. n. |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| nom. | manus | manūs | cornū | cornua |
| gen. | manūs | manuum | cornūs | cornuum |
| dat. | manuī | manibus | cornū | cornibus |
| acc. | manum | manūs | cornū | cornua |
| voc. | manus | manūs | cornū | cornua |
| abl. | manū | manibus | cornū | cornibus |

Gender: masculine, with a few feminines (*manus, domus, tribus, porticus, acus*) and the neuters (*cornū, genū, verū*). An older genitive in *-ī* (*senātī*) survives in early Latin; the dative singular *-ū* occurs in Caesar and others (*equitātū*). *Domus* mixes the second and fourth: *domus, domūs (domī), domuī, domum, domō/domū*; plural *domūs, domuum (domōrum), domibus, domōs/domūs*; the locative is *domī* "at home". Examples: *senatus* in *Senatus haec intellegit* (Cic. *Cat.* 1.2), "the senate understands this."

## 8. Fifth declension (ē-stems)

*rēs* f. "thing", *diēs* m./f. "day":

| | sg. | pl. | sg. | pl. |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| nom. | rēs | rēs | diēs | diēs |
| gen. | reī | rērum | diēī | diērum |
| dat. | reī | rēbus | diēī | diēbus |
| acc. | rem | rēs | diem | diēs |
| voc. | rēs | rēs | diēs | diēs |
| abl. | rē | rēbus | diē | diēbus |

The fifth declension is small. Only *rēs* and *diēs* have a full plural; *spēs, fidēs, aciēs, speciēs, effigiēs, seriēs* have the nominative and accusative plural and not the genitive and dative (*spērum* is rare) (Bennett 1918 §§51–53). The genitive and dative singular end in **-ēī** after a vowel (*diēī*, *aciēī*) and in **-eī** after a consonant (*reī, fideī, spe(ī)*). The nouns are feminine except *diēs* (masculine when it means a day in general, feminine for a fixed day or a period) and *merīdiēs* (masculine).

The phrase *rēs pūblica* shows the genitive in *rei publicae*: *satis facere rei publicae videmur* (Cic. *Cat.* 1.2); and the nominative in *res publica detrimenti caperet* (Cic. *Cat.* 1.4) in the formula of the *senātūs consultum ultimum*.

## 9. Irregular, defective and borrowed nouns

### 9.1 Heteroclites and heterogeneous nouns

*Heteroclite* nouns are declined in more than one pattern (Bennett 1918 §59): *domus* (II/IV), *requiēs* (III/V, gen. *requiētis, requiēī*), *plēbs* (*plēbēs*). *Heterogeneous* nouns differ in gender across the number: *locus* m., pl. *loca* n. (and *locī*); *iocus*, pl. *iocī, ioca*; *Tartarus*, *Tartara*; *caelum* n., *caelī* m. pl. (poetry).

### 9.2 Defective nouns

Some nouns lack cases: *fors* nom. only (and *forte*, abl.); *vicis* gen. (no nominative; *vicem, vice*; plural *vicēs*), *precī, precem, prece* (no nominative or genitive singular; Bennett 1918 §57, a); *nefās*, *fās* indeclinable; *opis* gen. (*ops*, nominative rare), *grātēs* (pl. only). The plural of abstract nouns can mean a type or a result (*ōtia* "leisure periods").

### 9.3 Indeclinable and borrowed nouns

Foreign names in Latin are declined if they fit and left alone if they do not. The Vulgate and later Christian Latin keep Hebrew names undeclined in many cases: *filii David, filii Abraham* (Matt 1:1), *Abraham genuit Isaac. Isaac autem genuit Jacob* (Matt 1:2), *ad Israël* (Mal 1:1); but *Judam* (Matt 1:2) shows a Latin accusative of a Hebrew name, as Judas, and the Greek *Jesu Christi* declined as Latin. The principle is not to inflect words that the text treats as foreign and to inflect the ones that have become Latin; this accounts for the Vulgate's unsteady appearance and is taken up in chapter 14.

### 9.4 The locative

The locative case marks the place where, in the names of cities, towns and small islands, and in a few common nouns. Its endings are: **-ae** (first sing.), **-ī** (second sing.), **-ī or -e** (third sing.), and **-īs** (plural): *Rōmae, Corinthī, Carthāginī/Carthāgine, Athēnīs, domī, humī, rūrī, bellī* ("in war"), *mīlitiae*, *vesperī* "in the evening" (Bennett 1918 §232). The dative and the ablative later took over its functions: *Corinthi* in a late text is a genitive or a dative; medieval charters use the ablative with *in* (*de loco Brancalo … in loco Metiano*, `la_llct` train-s2). The locative is the last of the seven Indo-European cases to survive.

## 10. The system compared: what the endings share

Here is the whole system in one table, of the stressed vowel and ending in the singular (the pattern the case-syncretism of the Late and Medieval language reduces further; chapters 14–15):

| case | I | II m. | II n. | III | IV | V |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| nom. sg. | -a | -us | -um | – | -us | -ēs |
| gen. sg. | -ae | -ī | -ī | -is | -ūs | -eī |
| dat. sg. | -ae | -ō | -ō | -ī | -uī | -eī |
| acc. sg. | -am | -um | -um | -em | -um | -em |
| abl. sg. | -ā | -ō | -ō | -e | -ū | -ē |
| nom. pl. | -ae | -ī | -a | -ēs | -ūs | -ēs |
| gen. pl. | -ārum | -ōrum | -ōrum | -um/-ium | -uum | -ērum |
| dat./abl. pl. | -īs | -īs | -īs | -ibus | -ibus | -ēbus |
| acc. pl. | -ās | -ōs | -a | -ēs | -ūs | -ēs |

Four syncretisms define the system: (1) dative = ablative in the plural of every declension; (2) neuter nominative = accusative = vocative; (3) accusative plural = nominative plural in the third, fourth and fifth; (4) nominative = vocative except in *-us* of the second. Their consequence is that a single form may be several cases, and the parser must disambiguate them by the agreement of adjective and verb, and by the verb's government: **the case is not a property of the word form but of its use in the sentence**. Of the nominative singular forms of the first declension (*rosa*) and the ablative (*rosā*) only the length separates them, and the written text hides it.

## 11. How Mova handles it

**The pipeline (2026-10-22).** The Latin pipeline consumes treebank-supplied morphology; it does not produce any. `en xpos-fill` rewrites each word's XPOS as a Penn-Treebank-style tag derived from the treebank's UPOS and FEATS — a noun becomes `NN`, or `NNS` if `Number=Plur`; a proper noun `NNP`/`NNPS` — and the rest of the pipeline (`en parse-eval`) is trained on those tags. **Case and Gender are not read by that step**; the LLCT train set carries them in FEATS (e.g. `Case=Abl|Gender=Masc|Number=Sing`), but the tag derived for the parser does not. On UD_Latin-LLCT the pipeline reaches LAS 87.35 (own tags) and 87.80 (gold tags) on dev, train → dev, 2026-10-22; the 0.45-point difference measures what the full original tags carry beyond the derived ones.

**UD conventions.** UPOS `NOUN`/`PROPN`; FEATS `Case`, `Gender`, `Number`; LLCT also keeps its own 10-position XPOS (`n|n|-|s|-|-|-|m|b|-`, in which the last but one position is the case: `b` = ablative, `g` = genitive, `a` = accusative). The Latin treebanks were harmonised in UPOS, FEATS and the main relations from UD 2.13 on (gamba-2023-latin-morphology-through; gamba-2023-universalising); they still differ in the XPOS: LLCT has the 10-position code above, PROIEL a short two-letter code for the part of speech (a common noun is `Nb`), ITTB a list of class, case and gender features, UDante a compact string of its own. A proper name in LLCT is `PROPN` with the dependency `flat` between its parts (`Carulo rege`: `appos`).

**Where it stops.** No Latin lexicon, no declension model, no analyser that proposes a case for a form. The most useful first step is not a model but a **table**: the endings above, matched against the LLCT forms, so that it can be counted how often a form is case-ambiguous (the dative/ablative plural, the nominative/accusative neuter, the *-a* of the first declension). That count is not made yet.
