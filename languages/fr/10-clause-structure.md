# 10. Clause structure and word order

The basic order of a French clause is **subject – verb – object**, as in English: *Le chat mange la souris.* What differs from English is everything around that core:
- the pronoun objects come before the verb ([chapter 4](04-pronouns.md));
- most adjectives follow the noun ([chapter 5](05-adjectives-adverbs.md));
- the subject must be expressed, so a clause with no real subject takes an empty *il*;
- speech marks topic and focus not by stress on a word in place, as English does, but by moving phrases out of the clause: dislocation, clefts.

This chapter describes the order of the clause and its parts, the impersonal constructions, and the constructions of information structure. The order inside the verb group (clitics, negation, auxiliaries) is in chapters [4](04-pronouns.md), [7](07-auxiliaries-participle.md) and [8](08-negation.md); questions are in [chapter 9](09-questions.md).

## 10.1 The core: subject, verb, objects

**The subject is obligatory** in a finite clause, except in the imperative. French is not a null-subject language: *Il pleut*, not \**Pleut*. Where there is no participant, the subject is the empty *il* (10.3).

**The direct object** follows the verb with no preposition, **the indirect object** follows it with *à* (or *de*), and a noun direct object normally comes before a noun indirect object:

> Il donne **le livre** **à sa sœur**. — "He gives the book to his sister."

A long or heavy direct object may follow the indirect one: *Il donne à sa sœur le livre qu'il venait d'acheter.* French has no dative shift: \**Il donne sa sœur le livre*.

**Pronoun objects** are clitics before the finite verb, or before the infinitive that governs them: *Il **le lui** donne.* — *Il veut **le lui** donner.* After a positive imperative they follow the verb: *Donne-**le-lui**.* See [chapter 4](04-pronouns.md).

**Adverbs.** A short adverb stands after the finite verb (*Il mange **souvent** du pain*); in a compound tense, between the auxiliary and the participle (*Il a **bien** mangé*). Unlike English, an adverb does not stand between the subject and the verb in the neutral order (\**Il souvent mange*). Sentence adverbs and adverbial phrases may open the clause (*Hier, il est parti*) or close it. Details are in [chapter 5](05-adjectives-adverbs.md).

**Complements of place, time and manner** follow the objects, normally in that order, with the heaviest last:

> Il a vu ses amis **au café** **hier soir**. — "He saw his friends at the café last night."

### Subject–verb inversion outside questions

The subject follows the verb, in writing:
- in incises of reported speech: *« Je viens », **dit-il**.* — *« Non », **répondit Marie**.*;
- after some opening adverbs (*peut-être, sans doute, aussi* "so", *ainsi, à peine, du moins, encore*), with complex inversion of a noun subject: ***Peut-être viendra-t-il.*** — ***Aussi le projet fut-il abandonné.*** Speech prefers *peut-être qu'il viendra*;
- in relative and comparative clauses with a short verb, a noun subject may follow it: *la maison qu'**habitait mon grand-père***; *plus grand que ne le croyait **son père***;
- after a locative or temporal phrase at the head of the clause, in narrative and description (stylistic inversion): ***Dans le jardin poussaient** des roses.*

## 10.2 The noun phrase

The order in the noun phrase is:

**determiner – (a few short adjectives) – noun – adjectives – complements with a preposition – relative clause**

> **le** [petit] **chat** [noir] [de ma voisine] [qui dort au soleil]
> "my neighbour's little black cat that sleeps in the sun"

A noun complement is a prepositional phrase, usually with *de*: *le livre **de Paul***, *une tasse **à café***, *un moulin **à vent***. French does not stack nouns as English does (*the town hall car park*): *le parking **de** la mairie*. The position of adjectives, and the meaning that changes with it (*un grand homme* / *un homme grand*), is in [chapter 5](05-adjectives-adverbs.md).

## 10.3 Impersonal constructions

An impersonal verb has the empty pronoun *il* as subject, with no referent:
- weather verbs: ***Il** pleut. **Il** neige. **Il** fait beau.*;
- *il y a* "there is, there are" (from *avoir*), *il faut* "it is necessary, one must", *il s'agit de* "it is a matter of":

> **fr-ud-dev_00717** · UD French GSD
> Il y a donc le monde de la réalité comprenant les nobles et les artisans et le monde de la magie.
> "So there is the world of reality, made up of the nobles and the craftsmen, and the world of magic."

> **fr-ud-dev_00776** · UD French GSD
> Il s'agit du premier Bound for Glory, …
> "It is the first Bound for Glory, …"

- an extraposed subject: the logical subject is a clause or a noun phrase after the verb, and *il* stands before it:

> **Il** est difficile **de répondre**. — "It is hard to answer."
> **Il** arrive **qu'il se trompe**. — "It happens that he is wrong."
> **Il** est arrivé **un accident**. — "An accident has happened." (The verb agrees with *il*, in the singular: ***Il** est arrivé **des** accidents.*)

In speech, *ce* (*c'*, *ça*) often replaces the empty *il* before an adjective: % ***C'**est difficile de répondre.* Speech also drops the *il* of *il faut* and *il y a*: % *Faut partir. — Y a personne.*

## 10.4 Dislocation

A phrase is placed outside the clause, at its left or right edge, and a pronoun inside the clause takes its place. This is the ordinary way of speech to set a topic:

> **Cette épreuve**, tu **la** fais costumé à cheval. (ParisStories, spoken)
> *this event, you it do in-costume on horse* — "This event, you ride it in costume."

> donc du coup, bah, **cette reprise elle** était magique. (ParisStories)
> "so, well, that ride, it was magic."

> **Moi**, je ne sais pas. — **Ton frère**, je **l'**ai vu hier. — Je **l'**ai vu hier, **ton frère**.

A dislocated subject is very frequent in speech, and a strong pronoun (*moi, toi, lui, eux*) is the usual way to stress a subject pronoun, which cannot carry stress itself: ***Moi**, je reste.* On our data (UD 2.18, 2026-10-22), the spoken ParisStories training set has 161 dislocated phrases in 22 540 words (7.1 per 1 000 words). The written GSD training set has 61 in 364 333 words (0.17 per 1 000): speech uses dislocation about forty times as often.

## 10.5 Clefts and pseudo-clefts

**The cleft** *c'est … qui / que* puts one phrase in focus. It is the French equivalent of English stress on a word:

> **fr-ud-dev_00594** · UD French GSD
> C'est l'autorité judiciaire elle-même qui reconnaît l'existence d'une erreur judiciaire.
> "It is the judicial authority itself that recognises the existence of a miscarriage of justice."

*Qui* follows a focused subject, *que* any other focused phrase: ***C'est** à Paris **que** je l'ai rencontré.* The verb after *qui* agrees with the focused phrase: *C'est moi qui **suis** venu.* *C'est* is normally invariable; *ce sont* before a plural noun belongs to careful style (*Ce sont eux qui …*).

**The pseudo-cleft** *ce qui / ce que …, c'est …* announces what is being focused:

> **Ce que** je veux, **c'est** partir. — "What I want is to leave."

> **fr-ud-dev_00907** · UD French GSD
> Ce qui justifierait les taches de sang du personnage.
> "Which would explain the bloodstains on the character." (A free relative standing alone as a sentence, taking up the sentence before.)

Speech also uses the presentative *il y a … qui*: % ***Il y a** quelqu'un **qui** t'attend* "There's someone waiting for you".

## 10.6 Speech: what the clause carries

Spoken French fills the clause with material written French leaves out:
- **discourse markers**: *bah, ben, bon, hein, quoi, enfin, du coup, genre*;
- **hesitations and repairs**: *euh*, a word begun again;
- **co-ordination** where writing subordinates.

In ParisStories (2026-10-22) the UD relation `discourse` occurs 1 614 times in 22 540 words, against 39 times in the 364 333 words of GSD; a corrected false start (`reparandum`) occurs 552 times. These are words of the spoken clause, not errors. A parser trained on writing alone does not expect them.

## How Mova handles it

**In the UD trees** (French, UD 2.18):
- **The empty *il*** of impersonal verbs is `PRON` attached as `expl:subj`, and an extraposed subject as `nsubj` or `csubj` of the predicate. The *se* of pronominal verbs is `expl:pv`, and the *y* of *il y a* is `expl:comp`. In the GSD development set these occur as `expl:pv` 104, `expl:subj` 86, `expl:pass` 55 and `expl:comp` 15 times.
- **A dislocated phrase** is `dislocated` (in ParisStories `dislocated:subj` and `dislocated:obj`), attached to the predicate, and the resumptive pronoun keeps its own relation (`nsubj`, `obj`).
- **A cleft** has the focused phrase as the root, *c'* as `expl:subj` and *est* as `cop` of the root, and the rest as `advcl:cleft` (181 times in the GSD training set).
- **Spoken material** is `discourse`, `reparandum` and `parataxis`.

**In Mova.** The parser learns these relations from the French trees through `en xpos-fill`. Mova's English frames reader already treats an expletive subject as no participant (*it rains*, *there is*) and reads an English cleft. For French it has neither yet. What is needed:
- `expl:subj` read as «no participant», so that *Il pleut* has no agent and *Il est arrivé un accident* has *accident* as its subject;
- a dislocated phrase joined to its resumptive pronoun, as one participant, so that *Cette épreuve, tu la fais* has *épreuve* as the object of *faire*;
- the cleft read as the plain clause with one phrase in focus: *C'est l'autorité qui reconnaît …* → *l'autorité reconnaît …*, focus *l'autorité*;
- `discourse` and `reparandum` set aside from the frames and kept for the pragmatics (hesitation, stance).

A parser trained on the written GSD alone does not know the spoken relations well. Adding the spoken treebanks ParisStories and Rhapsodie (CC BY-SA) to the training gave no gain on the written GSD (LAS 87.00 → 87.02, 2026-10-22); their effect on spoken text is not measured yet.
