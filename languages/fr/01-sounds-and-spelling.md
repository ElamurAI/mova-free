# 1. Sounds and spelling

French spelling is conservative and French pronunciation is not. The written word keeps letters that stopped being pronounced centuries ago, and the spoken chain of words changes shape at its joints: a final consonant that is silent in isolation comes back before a vowel (liaison), a final vowel disappears (elision), and an *e* comes and goes. This chapter describes the sounds, the way they are combined across word boundaries, the writing system, and the 1990 spelling rectifications. It is the chapter where the written and the spoken language are furthest apart, and the one where Mova, which reads text, has least to say.

## 1. The vowels

Standard French has about twelve oral vowels and three or four nasal vowels. The count varies with the speaker, because several oppositions are being lost.

| | front unrounded | front rounded | back rounded | central |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| close | /i/ *vie* | /y/ *vu* | /u/ *vous* | |
| close-mid | /e/ *blé* | /ø/ *peu* | /o/ *beau* | /ə/ *le* |
| open-mid | /ɛ/ *belle* | /œ/ *peur* | /ɔ/ *botte* | |
| open | /a/ *patte* (front), /ɑ/ *pâte* (back) | | | |

Nasal vowels: /ɛ̃/ *vin*, /ɑ̃/ *vent*, /ɔ̃/ *vont*, and /œ̃/ *brun*. The last is merged with /ɛ̃/ for most speakers in France, so that *brun* and *brin* are homophones, and is kept in Belgium and in parts of the south and of Québec. The two /a/ are merged in much of France as a single front or central vowel; /ɑ/ in *pâte* survives in Belgian, Québec and conservative Parisian speech. Passy (1906) and Nyrop (1902), working from the Paris pronunciation of their day, describe the distinctions that have since receded.

**Close-mid and open-mid.** The pairs /e/–/ɛ/, /ø/–/œ/, /o/–/ɔ/ are distributed by syllable type to a large extent. In a syllable that ends in a pronounced vowel (an open syllable) the close-mid vowel is normal: *peu* /pø/, *beau* /bo/, *blé* /ble/. In a syllable closed by a pronounced consonant the open-mid vowel is normal: *peur* /pœʁ/, *botte* /bɔt/, *belle* /bɛl/. This "law of position" (*loi de position*) has exceptions in each pair: a close-mid vowel stands in a closed syllable before /z/ and in a few words (*rose* /ʁoz/, *jeûne* /ʒøn/). In final open syllables the opposition is contrastive (*fée* /fe/ against *fait* /fɛ/; *chantai* /ʃɑ̃te/ against *chanterais* /ʃɑ̃tʁɛ/) but it is the one that is lost first: in the south and in much casual speech both words of such a pair are said with the same mid vowel.

**Length.** Vowels are phonetically long before /ʁ, z, ʒ, v, vʁ/ in a closed syllable (*mère*, *rose*, *neige*), and before a lengthening consonant. Length as a phonemic distinction (*mettre* /mɛtʁ/ against *maître* /mɛːtʁ/) is now rare in France and well preserved in Belgium, Switzerland and Québec.

**Semivowels.** Three glides occur before a vowel: /j/ (*bien*, *travail*, *yeux*), /w/ (*oui*, *moi*, *jouer*), /ɥ/ (*nuit*, *huit*, *lui*). The vowel–glide pair of *lier* /lje/ against *lire* /liʁ/ and of *nous louons* against *nous lions* is the only place where syllable counting separates the vowel from the glide (§3).

## 2. The consonants

| | labial | dental/alveolar | postalveolar | palatal | velar | uvular |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| stops | p b | t d | | | k ɡ | |
| fricatives | f v | s z | ʃ ʒ | | | ʁ |
| nasals | m | n | | ɲ | (ŋ) | |
| liquids | | l | | | | |
| glides | w ɥ | | | j | | |

French has 17 consonants plus the three glides in the usual count, with /ŋ/ only in loanwords (*camping*, *parking*) and /ɲ/ spelled *gn* (*montagne*). The phoneme /ʁ/ is uvular in standard French; the apical trill [r] survives in some regional speech, in Québec rural speech, in Belgium and in the singing tradition. Aspirated stops do not occur: /p t k/ are unaspirated and short, which is one of the first audible differences from English.

**Consonant clusters.** Initial clusters are of the type stop or fricative plus /ʁ/ or /l/ (*pris*, *blanc*, *fleur*, *gris*), with /s/ plus consonant in loans and learned words (*spécial*, *statue*). Final clusters are mostly liquid plus obstruent or obstruent plus liquid (*ours*, *table*, *quatre*, *peuple*). Where a cluster ending in /ʁ/ or /l/ occurs at the end of a word and the next word begins with a consonant, the liquid is often devoiced or dropped in rapid speech (*quatre* → [kat], *table* → [tab]); this is a feature of spoken French, not of the standard.

**Final consonants.** Most final consonant letters are silent (*petit*, *grand*, *temps*, *nez*, *est*), with a set of exceptions where they are pronounced (*sac*, *bref*, *sud*, *cinq*, *lac*, *mal*, *fils* /fis/). The consonants *c*, *r*, *f*, *l* (the mnemonic is "CaReFuL") are the ones most often pronounced at the end of a word, but no rule predicts a word: the matter is lexical. Words in *-er* are /e/ (*parler*, *premier*) except for a short list in /ɛʁ/ (*hiver*, *mer*, *fer*, *amer*, *cher*, *hier*, *enfer*).

## 3. Syllables, the mute *e*, and rhythm

French has no lexical stress. The only prominence is at the end of a rhythmic group (a group of words that belong together syntactically, typically of three to six syllables), where the last full syllable is lengthened and carries a pitch movement. In an isolated word it falls on the last syllable, which gives the impression of "stress on the end". A word is pronounced with the same weight on each syllable inside the group; this is the "syllable-timed" rhythm that speakers of English hear as staccato.

The **mute *e*** (*e muet*, *e caduc*, schwa) is written *e* and is pronounced /ə/ or /ø/ or not at all. The rule of thumb is the "law of three consonants": it is pronounced when its omission would leave three consonants together (*vendredi* /vɑ̃dʁədi/ in careful speech, *une petite fille* /yn(ə) pətit fij/) and dropped when the omission leaves two (*samedi* /samdi/, *la petite* /la ptit/). In practice the mute *e* is pronounced in the south, in verse and in careful reading, and dropped in running speech in the north, in Québec and in most casual speech. In verse of the classical tradition it counts as a syllable before a consonant, is silent before a vowel (elided) and at the end of the line; the conventions of French verse are tied to this practice (Martinon 1913 and Passy 1906 both treat the mute *e*).

The syllable structure favours open syllables: a majority of the syllables of running French end in a vowel. Sequences of vowels are mostly separated by a glide or by a syllable break (*créer* /kʁe.e/, *Noël* /nɔ.ɛl/); the diaeresis marks that two vowel letters are pronounced separately (§6).

## 4. Enchaînement and liaison

Two processes join a word to the one that follows when the second begins with a vowel.

**Enchaînement** is the resyllabification of a consonant that is pronounced at the end of the first word: *il arrive* /i.laʁiv/ (the /l/ of *il* belongs to the following syllable), *une amie* /y.na.mi/. It occurs with every pronounced final consonant and is optional only in the sense that the word boundary is not marked by a pause.

**Liaison** is the appearance, before a vowel (or mute *h*), of a final consonant that is silent when the word stands alone or before a consonant: *les amis* /le.za.mi/ (against *les gens* /le.ʒɑ̃/), *petit ami* /pə.ti.ta.mi/ (against *petit garçon* /pə.ti.ɡaʁ.sɔ̃/). The consonant is a different one from the letter's usual value in four cases: the letters *s*, *x*, *z* give /z/ (*deux hommes* /dø.zɔm/); *d* gives /t/ (*grand arbre* /ɡʁɑ̃.taʁbʁ/); *f* gives /v/ (*neuf ans* /nø.vɑ̃/, in this one expression); *g* gives /k/ (*long hiver* /lɔ̃.kivɛʁ/, now archaic). The letters *t*, *n*, *r*, *p* give /t/, /n/, /ʁ/, /p/.

Liaison is the main object of study in a body of work that stretches from Martinon (1913), who gives a detailed treatment, to the modern corpus work on spoken French. It falls into three classes, which are the standard presentation in every pronunciation manual:

- **Obligatory liaison** (*liaison obligatoire*): within a close grammatical unit, in particular between a determiner and a noun (*les amis*, *un ami* /œ̃.na.mi/ with /n/, *mes enfants*, *deux ans*), between an adjective that precedes and its noun (*petit ami*, *grand homme* /ɡʁɑ̃.tɔm/), between a pronoun clitic and the verb or another clitic (*ils ont* /il.zɔ̃/, *nous avons*, *vous êtes*, *on a*, *en a*, *y est*), after a monosyllabic preposition or adverb (*dans un*, *en Italie*, *sans eux*, *très aimable*, *plus intéressant* in the comparative), and in fixed expressions (*de plus en plus*, *c'est-à-dire*, *tout à fait*, *États-Unis*, *accent aigu*).
- **Forbidden liaison**: after *et* (*et alors*, with no /t/), before a *h aspiré* (*les héros* /le.ʁe.o/, *les haricots*, *un hibou*; but *les hommes* /le.zɔm/ with a mute *h*), between a plural noun subject and its verb (*les enfants arrivent* has no /z/), after a singular noun (*un enfant‿intelligent* has none), before *onze* and *oui*, .
- **Optional liaison**: the rest, and the part that is most sensitive to register. A verb followed by its complement (*ils sont‿arrivés*), a plural noun followed by an adjective (*des enfants‿intelligents*), a preposition of more than one syllable (*pendant‿un an*) all take liaison in a formal reading and take it much less in conversation. A liaison that is not in the standard (the *pataquès*, a wrong liaison consonant, as in *il est‿z‿arrivé*) is heard as an error.

The decision of whether to make a liaison is a property of the pair of words and of the register together, which is why it cannot be read from the spelling alone.

**The *h*.** French has two kinds of initial *h*, neither pronounced. The mute *h* (*h muet*) behaves as if the word began with the vowel: *l'homme*, *les hommes* /le.zɔm/. The aspirate *h* (*h aspiré*) blocks elision and liaison: *le héros*, *les héros* /le.ʁe.o/, *la haine*, *le haricot*. The aspirate *h* marks a closed lexical class, mostly words of Germanic origin or expressive words (*honte*, *hérisser*, *hurler*, *hasard*, *hache*); the dictionaries mark them.

## 5. Elision and contraction

**Elision** drops the vowel of a small set of function words before a word that begins with a vowel or a mute *h*, and writes an apostrophe:

| word | elided | before |
|---|---|---|
| *le*, *la* | *l'* | *l'homme*, *l'amie* |
| *je*, *me*, *te*, *se*, *ne*, *de*, *ce*, *que* | *j'*, *m'*, *t'*, *s'*, *n'*, *d'*, *c'*, *qu'* | *j'ai*, *n'a pas*, *c'est*, *qu'elle* |
| *si* | *s'* | only before *il(s)*: *s'il pleut*, but *si elle pleut* |
| *jusque*, *lorsque*, *puisque*, *quoique*, *presque* (only in *presqu'île*) | *jusqu'*, *lorsqu'*, *puisqu'*, *quoiqu'* | *jusqu'à*, *lorsqu'il*, *puisqu'on* |

*Ce* elides before forms of *être* and before *en* (*c'est*, *c'était*, *c'en est fini*); before other verbs it is not written elided in the standard (*ç'a été* is colloquial). The pronoun *tu* elides in speech (*t'as vu*, *t'es là*), and the spelling *t'* is written in informal text. There is no elision before an aspirate *h* (*le héros*), nor before *oui* or *onze* (*le oui*, *le onze*).

**Contraction** (*amalgame*) is the fusion of a preposition and the definite article:

| à + le | au | de + le | du |
|---|---|---|---|
| à + les | aux | de + les | des |

*À la* and *de la* are not contracted, and neither are the forms before a vowel (*à l'ami*, *de l'eau*). The relative forms *lequel*, *lesquels*, *lesquelles* fuse with *à* and *de* as well: *auquel*, *auxquels*, *auxquelles*, *duquel*, *desquels*, *desquelles*. The contracted forms are one written word and, in UD, a multiword token that stands for two syntactic words (§9).

## 6. The writing system: letters, digraphs, accents

The alphabet is the 26 letters of the Latin alphabet, with the ligatures *œ* (*œil*, *cœur*, *sœur*, *œuvre*) and *æ* (rare, in Latin loans: *et cætera*, *ex æquo*) counted as letters in the standard but not in the alphabetical order (they are sorted as *oe* and *ae*).

**Diacritics.**

| mark | letters | function |
|---|---|---|
| acute (*accent aigu*) | é | /e/ (*été*, *blé*) |
| grave (*accent grave*) | à, è, ù | /ɛ/ on *è* (*père*); on *à* and *ù* only a distinction of homonyms (*à* / *a*, *où* / *ou*, *là* / *la*) |
| circumflex (*accent circonflexe*) | â, ê, î, ô, û | a trace of a lost *s* (*forêt* < *forest*, *hôpital*) or of a lost vowel (*âge* < *aage*), a distinction of homonyms (*sûr* / *sur*, *dû* / *du*), a vowel quality or length |
| diaeresis (*tréma*) | ë, ï, ü, ÿ | the vowel is pronounced separately (*Noël*, *naïf*, *aiguë*, *Saül*) |
| cedilla (*cédille*) | ç | /s/ before *a*, *o*, *u* (*garçon*, *façade*, *reçu*) |

**Digraphs and trigraphs.** *ou* /u/, *oi* /wa/, *ai* /ɛ/ or /e/, *au*, *eau* /o/, *eu*, *œu* /ø/ or /œ/, *ch* /ʃ/, *gn* /ɲ/, *ph* /f/, *qu* /k/, *gu* /ɡ/ before *e*, *i*, *y*, *ill* /ij/ (*fille*, *travailler*) with the exceptions *ville*, *mille*, *tranquille* /il/, nasal vowels written with a vowel plus *n* or *m* (*an*, *en*, *in*, *on*, *un*; before *b* and *p* the *m* is written: *lampe*, *timbre*).

**The values of *c*, *g*, *s*.** *c* is /k/ before *a*, *o*, *u* or a consonant, and /s/ before *e*, *i*, *y*; *g* is /ɡ/ in the same environments, and /ʒ/ before *e*, *i*, *y*; *s* is /z/ between vowels and /s/ elsewhere, which is why *rose* and *rosse* differ in /z/ and /s/, and why a verb with the stem *-g-* (*manger*) adds an *e* before *a* and *o* to keep the soft sound (*mangeons*, *mangeait*).

**Nasalisation.** A vowel letter followed by *n* or *m* is nasal if the *n* or *m* is followed by a consonant or is final (*banc*, *bon*, *faim*); the vowel is oral and the consonant is pronounced if the consonant is doubled or followed by a vowel (*bonne*, *bonté*, *animal*, *inouï*). The prefix cases go the other way: *ennemi* /ɛnmi/ has an oral vowel and a pronounced /n/, *emmener* /ɑ̃mne/ a nasal vowel and a pronounced /m/.

## 7. Silent letters and homophones

Because the spelling preserves old forms, French has a large number of **homophones**, many of them distinguished only by the spelling and by the grammatical context: *vert* / *verre* / *vers* / *ver* /vɛʁ/; *saint* / *sein* / *seing* / *ceint* /sɛ̃/; *cent* / *sang* / *sans* / *sent* /sɑ̃/. Where the grammar is concerned the important cases are the endings:

- the verb endings *-e*, *-es*, *-ent* of the present of verbs in *-er* are all silent: *il parle* = *ils parlent* = *tu parles* /paʁl/;
- *-ez* is /e/ as in *vous parlez*, which is also the sound of *-é*, *-ée*, *-er*, *-ai*: the past participle *parlé*, the infinitive *parler* and the second plural *parlez* are homophones, and so are the first singular *je parlai* (past simple) and the participle; the choice among them is a grammatical decision ([ch. 6](06-verb.md) and 7);
- the plural *-s* of the noun and the adjective is silent: *le chat* / *les chats* differ in the determiner only ([ch. 2](02-noun.md));
- *ses* / *ces* / *c'est* / *s'est* / *sait*, *quand* / *quant* / *qu'en*, *a* / *à*, *et* / *est*, *on* / *ont*, *son* / *sont*, *ou* / *où*, *la* / *là*, *mais* / *mes* / *met*: the French reader chooses by the syntax.

The homophone problem matters for any system that works from sound (speech recognition, text to speech) and for any system that learns from spelling: for a text-based parser the homophones are disambiguated by the spelling, which makes the written form an easier object than the spoken.

## 8. The 1990 rectifications

In 1990 the Conseil supérieur de la langue française presented to the Académie française a set of spelling "rectifications", published in the *Journal officiel* of 6 December 1990 (cslf-1990). The Académie française heard the proposals in its session of 3 May 1990 and approved them unanimously (cslf-1990), and the Québec language council agreed. They are a recommendation, not an obligation; the report itself states that no adult will be required to give up the traditional spelling. The text that we hold lists seven rules and a larger list of individual spellings.

The seven rules (cslf-1990, part II):

1. **Hyphen in numerals.** Compound numerals are joined by hyphens, below and above a hundred: *vingt-trois* (already), *cent-trois*, *deux-cents*, *deux-cent-soixante-et-onze*, *sept-cent-mille-trois-cent-vingt-et-un*.
2. **Plural of compounds.** Compounds made of a verb and a noun, or of a preposition and a noun, follow the rule of simple words: the mark of the plural is on the second element and only in the plural: *un cure-dent, des cure-dents*, *un pèse-lettre, des pèse-lettres*, *un après-midi, des après-midis*. Exceptions: a capitalised noun or a noun preceded by a singular article stays unmarked (*des prie-Dieu*, *des trompe-l'œil*).
3. **Grave accent.** (a) Futures and conditionals of verbs like *céder* take the grave like *semer*: *je cèderai*, *j'allègerai*. (b) The inverted first person of the present in *-e* takes the grave: *aimè-je*, *puissè-je*.
4. **Circumflex.** It is kept on *a*, *e*, *o* and is no longer obligatory on *i* and *u*, with the exceptions: the endings of the past simple (*nous suivîmes*, *vous voulûtes*), the imperfect and pluperfect subjunctive (*qu'il fût*, *qu'il eût aimé*), and some words (*dû*, *jeûne*, *mûr*, *sûr*, *croître*): so *il plait*, *traine*, *traitre*, *piqure*, *ambigument*, *dument*, *mu* (the report gives these as regular parallels of the already unaccented *tait*, *fait*, *gaine*, *su*, *vu*, *lu*). The rule itself also allows *maitre*, *ile*, *cout* (the circumflex on *i* and *u* is no longer obligatory), though these are not among the words the report spells out. Proper names keep it (*Nîmes*).
5. **Verbs in *-eler* and *-eter*.** The grave accent marks the open /ɛ/ for all such verbs, except for *appeler*, *jeter* and their families: *elle ruissèle*, *il étiquètera*, *j'époussète*, against *j'appelle*, *je jette*.
6. **Participle of *laisser*.** The past participle of *laisser* before an infinitive is invariable: *elle s'est laissé mourir*, *je les ai laissé partir*, *la maison qu'elle a laissé saccager* (like the already-invariable *fait* in the same position).
7. **Loanwords.** Nouns and adjectives of foreign origin have a regular singular and plural: *un scénario, des scénarios*, *un ravioli, des raviolis*, *un match, des matchs*, *un maximum, des maximums*; those ending in *s*, *x*, *z* are unchanged (*des boss*). Foreign compounds are soldered (*weekend*, *hotdog*, *covergirls*).

Besides the rules, the report gives lists of individual spellings (the "graphies"), among them *ognon* (for *oignon*), *nénufar* (for *nénuphar*), *relai* (for *relais*), *charriot* (for *chariot*), *combattif* (for *combatif*), *persiffler* (for *persifler*), *dissout* (for *dissous*), *assoir* (for *asseoir*), *exéma* (for *eczéma*), *pagaille* (for *pagaïe*) and *évènement* (for *événement*, under the accent rule).

The rectified spellings are used by some publishers and teachers (we have not measured their share); the published French we hold, including the treebanks of the UD release, uses the traditional spelling almost entirely. A parser must therefore be ready for either spelling: *événement* and *évènement*, *oignon* and *ognon*, *il connaît* and *il connait* are the same word.

## 9. How Mova handles it

**What is done.** Mova works from the written form. It has no phonological component for French: no grapheme-to-phoneme rules, no liaison model, no syllabification. The text is read as written and the tokenisation follows the UD French conventions.

**Tokenisation in the UD French GSD treebank** (UD 2.18, counted 2026-10-22):

- an elided function word is a token of its own, with the apostrophe attached to it: `l'` `homme`, `qu'` `ils`, `d'` `aujourd'hui` (fr-ud-dev_00549). The word *aujourd'hui* itself is one token (120 occurrences in GSD) and not split at the apostrophe.
- a contraction is a **multiword token**: the surface form *aux* is a range line over two syntactic words, `à` (ADP, `case`) and `les` (DET, `det`) (fr-ud-dev_00007, *aux tueurs*). In GSD *du* occurs 4 377 times, *des* (contracted) 3 294, *au* 2 338, *aux* 797, *auquel* 20, *duquel* 15 (as range lines, counted over the three files). A tool that tokenises French must therefore either split these forms or accept that the dependency is drawn to the second syllable: this is the one tokenisation step that French needs and English does not.
- the hyphenated inverted pronoun is split from the verb and keeps its hyphen on the pronoun: *Va-t-il* is `Va` + `-t-il` (fr-ud-dev_00602); *Pouvez-vous* is `Pouvez` + `-vous` (fr-ud-train_02355). The pronoun of the second token is `nsubj` of the first.
- compound words with a hyphen are one token (*c'est-à-dire*, a `CCONJ`, fr-ud-train_04834; *Vieux-Flassans*, fr-ud-dev_00188), as are the numerals with a hyphen (*vingt-neuf*). A number written with a space as thousands separator is one token (*25 785* is one `NUM`, fr-ud-dev_00092), so a whitespace tokeniser would split it wrongly.

**Liaison data.** The open spoken treebank UD French ParisStories carries phonological information in the MISC and FEATS columns: `LiaisonPossibleBefore=Yes` (766 words in the files, 2026-10-22), `LiaisonAfter=Yes` (526), and `HasSpokenGender`, `HasSpokenNumber` features that record whether a word's gender or number is audible (for instance *OnlySingExceptWithLiaison*, 2 490 words; *OnlyWithLiaison*, 1 108 words). It is the only open source known to us that links the written token to its liaison behaviour. Mova does not read these columns yet.

**Spelling variants.** There is no list of rectified spellings and no normaliser; a rectified form such as *ognon* or *nénufar* is an unknown word to the tagger. The 1990 rules are regular enough (rule 4 above, the circumflex on *i* and *u*; rule 3, the grave in futures) to be applied as a small set of rewrite rules in a normaliser, and the wanted list in [sources.md](sources.md) names the full word list.

**What is not handled.** No phonemes, no stress or intonation, no liaison model, no homophone model, no normalisation of the rectified spelling, no syllable counting (needed for verse). The tagger's accuracy on words it has not seen is 84.8% (2026-10-22), and a spelling variant counts as unseen.

**Sources.** Passy 1906 (passy-1906), Nyrop 1902 (nyrop-1902), Martinon 1913 (martinon-1913) for the phonetics and liaison; the 1990 text (cslf-1990) for the rectifications; UD French GSD and ParisStories for the tokenisation and the liaison features.
