# 8. Negation

Standard French negates a clause with two words. The first is *ne*, a clitic placed before the finite verb, together with any object clitics. The second is a word that says what kind of negation it is: *pas* («not»), *plus* («no longer»), *jamais* («never»), *rien* («nothing»), *personne* («nobody»), *aucun* («no, none»), *nulle part* («nowhere»), *guère* («hardly»), *point* («not at all», archaic or regional). In speech the *ne* is mostly dropped, and the second word carries the negation alone. This chapter describes the written standard first, then what speech does, then the cases where *ne* is not negative at all.

## 8.1 *ne … pas*: the position of the two parts

*Ne* stands immediately before the finite verb. Only the object clitics may come between them ([chapter 4](04-pronouns.md)). *Pas* stands immediately after the finite verb.

> Je **ne** sais **pas**.
> *I NEG know not* — "I don't know."

> Il **ne** le lui donne **pas**.
> *he NEG it to-him gives not* — "He doesn't give it to him."

Before a vowel or a mute *h*, *ne* elides to *n'*:

> Elle **n'**aime **pas** le café.
> *she NEG likes not the coffee* — "She doesn't like coffee."

**Compound tenses.** The finite verb is the auxiliary, so the two parts surround it, and the participle comes after *pas*:

> Je **n'**ai **pas** compris.
> *I NEG have not understood* — "I didn't understand."

**Infinitives.** With an infinitive the two parts stand together before it (and before its clitics):

> Il préfère **ne pas** répondre.
> *he prefers NEG not answer-INF* — "He prefers not to answer."

> Prière de **ne pas** le déranger.
> *request of NEG not him disturb-INF* — "Please do not disturb him."

The older order *n'être pas*, *ne l'avoir pas vu*, with *pas* after an infinitive of *être* or *avoir*, is still found in careful literary prose. It is correct but marked.

**Questions with inversion** ([chapter 9](09-questions.md)). The subject clitic follows the verb, and *pas* follows the subject clitic:

> **Ne** viens-tu **pas** ?
> *NEG come-you not* — "Aren't you coming?"

> **N'**a-t-il **pas** déjà payé ?
> *NEG has-he not already paid* — "Hasn't he already paid?"

## 8.2 The second word: what kind of negation

| second word | meaning | place | example |
|---|---|---|---|
| *pas* | not | after the finite verb | *Il ne vient pas.* "He isn't coming." |
| *plus* | no longer, no more | after the finite verb | *Il ne vient plus.* "He doesn't come any more." |
| *jamais* | never | after the finite verb | *Il ne vient jamais.* "He never comes." |
| *guère* | hardly, scarcely | after the finite verb | *Il ne vient guère.* "He hardly ever comes." |
| *point* | not at all (literary, regional) | after the finite verb | *Il ne vient point.* |
| *rien* | nothing | object: after the finite verb; subject: before *ne* | *Il ne voit rien.* / *Rien ne bouge.* |
| *personne* | nobody | object: after the participle; subject: before *ne* | *Il n'a vu personne.* / *Personne ne vient.* |
| *aucun, aucune* | no, not any | as a determiner or a pronoun | *Il n'a aucune idée.* / *Aucun ne répond.* |
| *nul, nulle* | no (formal) | as a determiner or a pronoun | *Nul n'est censé ignorer la loi.* |
| *nulle part* | nowhere | after the participle | *Il ne va nulle part.* |
| *ni … ni* | neither … nor | before each coordinated term | *Il ne boit ni vin ni bière.* |

Two of them, *personne* and *nulle part*, follow the participle in compound tenses. The adverbs follow the auxiliary:

> Je **n'**ai **jamais** vu ça. — "I have never seen that."
> Je **n'**ai vu **personne**. — "I didn't see anybody."
> Je **n'**ai **rien** vu. — "I didn't see anything."

*Rien* behaves like an adverb here: it stands between the auxiliary and the participle.

**A negative word as the subject.** *Personne*, *rien*, *aucun* and *nul* can be the subject. They then come first, *ne* still precedes the verb, and there is no *pas*:

> **Personne ne** répond. — "Nobody answers."
> **Rien n'**a changé. — "Nothing has changed."

### Several negative words together

The second words combine, with one *ne*, and the meaning stays negative. Two negatives do not cancel each other:

> Il **ne** dit **jamais rien** à **personne**.
> *he NEG says never nothing to nobody* — "He never says anything to anyone."

> Je **ne** le verrai **plus jamais**. — "I'll never see him again."

*Pas* does not combine with *rien*, *personne*, *jamais* or *plus* in the standard (\**il ne dit pas rien*). In speech *pas rien* and *pas personne* mean «not nothing», «not nobody»: the negation then really is double. *Pas un*, «not one», is standard.

### Negative words without a verb

In an answer or a fragment, the second word alone is negative:

> — Qui est venu ? — **Personne.** "— Who came? — Nobody."
> — Tu en veux ? — **Jamais de la vie !** "— Do you want some? — Not on your life!"
> — **Pas** moi. "Not me."

*Non* is the negative answer and the negation of a word or a phrase without a verb:

> — Tu viens ? — **Non.**
> Il est content, moi **non**. "He's pleased; I'm not."
> une solution **non** violente — "a non-violent solution"

*Si* answers a negative question positively («— Tu ne viens pas ? — Si !»), where *oui* would sound wrong.

## 8.3 *ne … que*: restriction, not negation

*Ne … que* means «only». The sentence is positive, not negative. *Que* stands immediately before the word or phrase it restricts, wherever that is:

> **fr-ud-dev_01038** · UD French GSD
> Les américains ne les rejoindront que quatre ans plus tard avec le programme Mariner.
> *the Americans NEG them will-join only four years more late with the program Mariner* — "The Americans would only join them four years later, with the Mariner programme."

> Il **ne** boit **que** de l'eau. — "He drinks only water."

Because the sentence is positive, the article after it is not reduced to *de* (8.5): *il ne boit que **de l'**eau*, not \**que d'eau*.

*Ne … pas que* is «not only»: *Il n'y a pas que ça* "That's not all there is." The cleft *ce n'est que … que* restricts a whole phrase:

> **fr-ud-dev_01310** · UD French GSD
> Ce n'est qu'à l'issue de ce délai que l'ordonnance pourra être notifiée au prévenu.
> "Only once this period is over can the order be served on the accused."

## 8.4 Speech: *ne* dropped

In ordinary spoken French, in all regions and at all social levels, *ne* is mostly left out. *Pas*, *plus*, *jamais*, *rien* and *personne* carry the negation alone:

> % Je sais **pas**. (written: *Je ne sais pas*.)
> % Il vient **plus**.
> % Y a **personne**. (*Il n'y a personne.*)

On our data, in the spoken UD French ParisStories training set (UD 2.18, counted 2026-10-22), 32 *ne* or *n'* stand against 206 second words (*pas* 196, *que* 9, *plus* 1). In the written UD French GSD development set, 127 *ne* or *n'* stand against 117 second words. So *ne* is kept in about one negated clause in seven in that spoken corpus, and in nearly all of them in the written one.

Dropping *ne* is not a sign of carelessness; it is the normal spoken norm. The written standard keeps *ne*, and so do formal speech, broadcast news and reading aloud. [Chapter 13](13-varieties-registers.md) gives the other differences between spoken and written French.

**Where *ne* is more often kept in speech:** with *rien*, *personne* and *aucun* as subjects (*personne ne sait*). It is almost always kept there, because without it the clause has no negative word before the verb and could be heard as positive. It is also kept in set phrases (*n'importe qui*, *ne serait-ce que*) and in careful style.

**Ne-drop and *que*.** Without *ne*, *que* «only» becomes ambiguous with other *que*s. Speech often uses *seulement* or *juste* instead: % *Il boit juste de l'eau.*

## 8.5 *de* after negation

Under negation, a direct object introduced by the indefinite article (*un, une, des*) or the partitive (*du, de la, des*) takes *de* instead ([chapter 3](03-determiners.md)):

> J'ai **un** vélo. → Je n'ai **pas de** vélo.
> Il boit **du** vin. → Il ne boit **pas de** vin.

> **fr-ud-dev_00353** · UD French GSD
> La fille qui répond n'a pas de temps à nous consacrer et est impatiente.
> "The girl who answers has no time to give us and is impatient."

The rule holds for a direct object under *pas*, *plus*, *jamais*, *guère* and *point*. It does not hold:
- after *être*: *Ce n'est pas **un** problème*;
- with a definite article: *Je n'aime pas **le** café*;
- under *ne … que*, which is not negative (8.3);
- when the negation bears on something else and the object exists: *Je n'ai pas acheté **une** voiture, j'en ai acheté deux*.

## 8.6 Expletive *ne*

In some subordinate clauses a *ne* appears without a second word and without any negative meaning. Traditional grammars call it *ne explétif*. It is optional, belongs to careful written style and is usually left out in speech:
- after verbs of fearing: *Je crains qu'il **ne** vienne* "I'm afraid he may come" (he may come; it is not «that he may not come»);
- after *avant que*, *à moins que*, *sans que* (the last one disputed): *avant qu'il **ne** parte* "before he leaves";
- after a comparative of inequality: *Il est plus grand que je **ne** pensais* "He is taller than I thought."

A negative fear needs *ne … pas*: *Je crains qu'il **ne** vienne **pas*** "I'm afraid he won't come."

The expletive *ne* after verbs of fearing continues the Latin *nē* of fear clauses (*timeō nē veniat* "I fear he may come": [la chapter 10](../la/10-subordination.md), §4). Latin itself had no negative concord: two negatives cancel ([la chapter 8](../la/08-prepositions-particles.md), §7).

## 8.7 Scope

The negation of *ne … pas* covers the clause. What it denies in particular is often only what is new or stressed:

> Il **n'**est **pas** venu en voiture (, il est venu à pied).

Constituent negation without a verb uses *non* or *pas*:

> une victoire **non** méritée; **pas** toujours; **pas** du tout ("not at all"); **non** sans mal ("not without difficulty").

*Sans* «without» carries a negation of its own. It takes *de* like a negation, or no article at all: *sans argent* "without money". With an infinitive it gives *sans + infinitive* (*sans rien dire* "without saying anything", where *rien* needs no *ne*):

> **fr-ud-dev_00340** · UD French GSD
> Les serveuses et serveurs sont accueillant sans être trop présents.
> "The waitresses and waiters are welcoming without being too present." (The text's missing plural *accueillants* is kept as published.)

## How Mova handles it

**In the UD trees.** UD French GSD marks negation on the words, not as a separate layer:
- *ne*/*n'* and the negative adverbs (*pas*, *plus*, *jamais*, *point*, *guère*) are `ADV` with `Polarity=Neg`, each attached to the verb as `advmod`;
- the *que* of *ne … que* is also `ADV` `Polarity=Neg` `advmod`, but it attaches to the phrase it restricts (in *ne les rejoindront que quatre ans plus tard*, to *tard*), not to the verb;
- the negative pronouns *rien* and *personne* are `PRON` in their argument relation (`nsubj`, `obj`);
- *non* is `ADV` (or `INTJ` as an answer).

In the GSD development set (2026-10-22) the words with `Polarity=Neg` are: *pas* 89, *n'* 69, *ne* 59, *que/qu'* 21, *non* 9, *plus* 7, *jamais* 2, *point* 1.

**In Mova.** French goes through Mova's English parser after `en xpos-fill`, which gives these words the tag `RB` (an adverb). The parser learns their attachment from the French training trees, so *ne* and *pas* are attached as `advmod` of the verb as in GSD. What Mova does not have yet:
- **The meaning of the two-part negation.** Mova's frames reader (`prag`) takes negation from English words (*not*, *n't*, *never*, *no*). It does not know that the pair *ne … pas* negates the frame, that *ne … que* does not, or that a lone *pas*, *plus* or *jamais* negates in speech. Until it reads French, a French frame is not marked negative.
- **Expletive *ne*** is not told from negative *ne*. In the trees both are `advmod` with `Polarity=Neg`, so UD itself does not separate them. A rule must look at the governing word (*craindre*, *avant que*, a comparative) and at the missing second word.
- **The *de* after negation** is a `det` of its noun in GSD (`Definite=Ind`, [chapter 3](03-determiners.md)), and the parser learns that; nothing yet checks it against the negation (\**Il n'a pas des amis*).

These are the first things the French frames reader needs, in that order.
