# Genitive without a noun: at the baker's, St Paul's, It's John's

**Gist.** A genitive can stand without the noun it belongs to. Poutsma distinguishes three cases: the noun has just been mentioned (*My car is faster than John's*); a house, shop, firm or church is meant (*at the baker's, at my aunt's, St Paul's*); a predicative genitive (*The book is John's*).
**Conditions and exceptions.** A classifying genitive (*a lady's umbrella*) is hardly ever used without the noun — the noun is repeated: *a gentleman's umbrella and a lady's umbrella*. Replacing it with *that of John* for a simple name is "not in the spirit of the language" (Poutsma).
**Examples.** *We met at Mary's.*; *I ate at McDonald's.*; *This pen is Tom's.*
**In UD.** The possessor noun takes the role of the whole phrase — `obl`, `nsubj`, `obj`, predicate root — and `case('s)` stays attached to it. EWT 2.18: POS on a head that is not `nmod:poss` — 88 times (`obl` 19, `nsubj` 11, `root` 8…). The rule is not formalized: such a POS is legitimate, and only the absence of a noun distinguishes it from `nmod:poss`.
**Sources.** Poutsma GLME vol. 3, Ch. XXIV §45–49 (pp. 117–119 = book pp. 97–99); Curme 1931 §10 II (pp. 70–88).
